Station 1 · Statistics

Parts of a whole: percentages, bar graphs and pie charts

The idea

Categorical data puts each item into a group (Fruit, Nuts, …). Every group is a fraction of the total. A percentage and a pie angle are the same fraction, measured in different units:

$$\begin{gathered}\text{percentage}=\frac{\text{frequency}}{\text{total}}\times 100\%\\[4pt]\text{pie angle}=\frac{\text{frequency}}{\text{total}}\times 360^\circ\end{gathered}$$

A bar graph compares the sizes of groups directly. A pie chart shows each group as a share of the whole. Both need a title and labels.
Trap: “the biggest group” is not the same as “more than half”. A group is a majority only when its share is more than $50\%$, which means an angle of more than $180^\circ$.
Play · build the charts

Move the sliders. Watch the percentages and angles change, and check whether the largest group is really a majority.

Worked example · Paper Question 1 (snack packs)

80 snack packs: Fruit 28, Nuts 20, Crackers 24, Popcorn 8.

  1. Fruit percentage: $\dfrac{28}{80}\times100\%=35\%$.
  2. Fruit angle: $\dfrac{28}{80}\times360^\circ=126^\circ$.
  3. In the same way: Nuts $25\%$, $90^\circ$; Crackers $30\%$, $108^\circ$; Popcorn $10\%$, $36^\circ$.
  4. Check: $35+25+30+10=100\%$ and $126+90+108+36=360^\circ$.
  5. The claim “the best-selling type must be more than half” is false. Fruit sells the most, but $35\%<50\%$.
Why does the angle formula work?

A full turn is $360^\circ$, and it stands for the whole group. If a group is $\frac{f}{T}$ of the whole, its sector must be $\frac{f}{T}$ of the full turn, which is $\frac{f}{T}\times360^\circ$.

That also gives a quick conversion: $1\%$ of a pie is $3.6^\circ$, so $35\%$ is $35\times3.6^\circ=126^\circ$.

It works backwards too. A $72^\circ$ sector is $\frac{72}{360}=\frac15$ of the pie, so in a survey of 250 people it stands for $\frac15\times250=50$ people.

Station 2 · Statistics

Five-number summary, box plots and the IQR

The idea

Put the data in order. Five numbers then describe the whole spread (lowest value, lower quartile, median, upper quartile, highest value):

$$\text{lowest}\le\text{LQ}\le\text{median}\le\text{UQ}\le\text{max}$$

  • The median is the middle value. For $n$ values it is in position $\frac{n+1}{2}$.
  • The lower quartile (LQ) is the median of the lower half. The upper quartile (UQ) is the median of the upper half.
  • When $n$ is odd, leave out the middle value when you make the two halves. When $n$ is even, split the list exactly in two.
$\text{IQR}=\text{UQ}-\text{LQ}$ is the spread of the middle half of the data. Unlike the range, it ignores extreme values.
Trap: always order the data first. Also, the quartiles are positions in the ordered list, not a quarter of the values.
Play · find the five numbers
Worked example · Paper Question 2 (Class A bag masses)

Class A, in order (kg): $2,\ 2.5,\ 3,\ 3,\ 3.5,\ 4,\ 4.5,\ 5,\ 5,\ 6,\ 7$. There are $n=11$ values.

  1. Median: position $\frac{11+1}{2}=6$, so the median is $4$ kg.
  2. Lower half (leave out the median): $2,\ 2.5,\ 3,\ 3,\ 3.5$, so $\text{LQ}=3$ kg.
  3. Upper half: $4.5,\ 5,\ 5,\ 6,\ 7$, so $\text{UQ}=5$ kg.
  4. Five-number summary: $2,\ 3,\ 4,\ 5,\ 7$. $\text{IQR}=5-3=2$ kg.
  5. Class B works the same way: $3,\ 4,\ 5.5,\ 6.5,\ 8$, and $\text{IQR}=6.5-4=2.5$ kg.

So Class B has a higher median ($5.5$ kg against $4$ kg) and its middle half is more spread out ($2.5$ kg against $2$ kg).

Why does a box plot split the data into quarters?

The median splits the ordered data into two halves. The quartiles split each half again. So the four parts of a box plot (left whisker, left box, right box, right whisker) each hold about a quarter of the data.

A long part does not mean more data. It means the same quarter of the data is more spread out.

Station 3 · Statistics

Comparing two groups: the 75%–50% rule

The idea

Two samples nearly always overlap, so we never say “every B is bigger than every A”. Instead we ask whether B tends to be bigger.

75%–50% rule. Take the upper quartile of group A. (About 75% of A is at or below it.) If more than 50% of group B is above A's upper quartile, we can say: “B tends to be greater than A.” This happens when B's median is above A's upper quartile.

Then back up your answer with a median (centre) or an IQR (spread), and give the numbers with units.

Trap: “tends to be heavier” is a claim about the groups in general. One heavy bag in A and one light bag in B do not break it, but they do break “every B is heavier”.
Play · slide Class B and test the rule

Class A stays fixed. Move every Class B bag lighter or heavier and watch the share of B above A's upper quartile.

Worked example · Paper Question 3
  1. Class A's upper quartile is $5$ kg.
  2. Class B values strictly above $5$: $5.5,\ 6,\ 6.5,\ 6.5,\ 7,\ 8$, which is $6$ of the $11$ values.
  3. $\dfrac{6}{11}\times100\%\approx54.5\%>50\%$, so the rule supports “Class B students tend to carry heavier bags”.
  4. “Every Class B bag is heavier than every Class A bag” is not supported. Counterexample: one B bag is $3$ kg and one A bag is $7$ kg.
Why 75% and 50%?

About 75% of group A is at or below A's upper quartile. If more than half of group B is above that line, then most of B sits higher than three quarters of A. A shift that big is hard to explain by chance alone with samples of this size, so we say “B tends to be” greater. We do not say “always”.

Example. A's UQ is 31 and B's median is 34. More than half of B is above 34, so more than half of B is above 31. The rule says yes.

Example. A's UQ is 31 and B's median is 29. Fewer than half of B are above 31. The rule does not support the claim, even though B's median might still be a little higher than A's.

Station 4 · Statistics

Scatter plots and the line of best fit

The idea

A scatter plot shows pairs of measurements, such as (length, width) for each leaf. To describe it, use three words:

  • Direction: positive (both go up together) or negative (one goes up, the other goes down).
  • Strength: strong (points close to a line), moderate, or weak.
  • Unusual points: points far from the pattern.
A line of best fit follows the trend, with points roughly balanced above and below it. Use it to estimate inside the data range (interpolation). Outside the range (extrapolation) the estimate is unreliable, because the pattern may not continue.
Trap: repeated points sit on top of each other. Mark them (for example with a small “2”) so no leaf is lost.
Play · fit your own line to the 20 leaves
Worked example · Paper Question 4 (leaves)
  1. The 20 points rise from left to right, so the relationship is positive. Most points lie close to a line, so it is moderately strong overall.
  2. $(15,\,3)$ is unusual: that leaf is much narrower than the pattern suggests.
  3. Example line: $\text{width}\approx0.5\times\text{length}+0.25$.
  4. At length $10.5$ cm: $0.5\times10.5+0.25=5.5$ cm. Anything from about $5$ to $6$ cm fits a reasonable line.
  5. At $30$ cm the line is not reliable. $30$ is far outside the measured lengths ($4$ to $17$ cm).
Why is extrapolation risky? Two examples

Leaves: the line predicts $0.5\times30+0.25=15.25$ cm for a $30$ cm leaf. No leaf near that size was measured, and very long leaves of this plant may be narrow, or may not exist at all.

Growth: a child grows about 6 cm a year between ages 4 and 10. The same line would predict a 40-year-old over 2.5 m tall. Patterns change outside the data.

Station 5 · Statistics

Reading graphs carefully: misleading axes

The idea

A bar's height should be in proportion to its value. When the vertical axis starts above $0$, the bars are cut off at the bottom, and a small change can look huge.

Always read the numbers on the axis, not the picture. Describe a change with an actual difference, and with a percentage change: $$\text{percentage change}=\frac{\text{new}-\text{old}}{\text{old}}\times100\%$$
Trap: “tripled” means the new value is $3\times$ the old value. If the bar only looks three times as tall, check where the axis starts.
Play · move where the axis starts
Worked example · Paper Question 5 (foodbank)
  1. Week 1: $34$ crates. Week 2: $38$ crates. Increase: $38-34=4$ crates.
  2. Graph B starts at $32$, so the visible bars are $34-32=2$ and $38-32=6$ units tall. Week 2 looks three times as big.
  3. “Donations tripled” is wrong. A supported claim: “Donations rose by 4 crates, from 34 to 38, an increase of about $11.8\%$.”
  4. $\dfrac{38-34}{34}\times100\%\approx11.8\%$

Station 6 · Probability

Theoretical probability, “or”, and the probability scale

The idea

When all outcomes are equally likely (a fair spinner, a fair die, a random card):

$$P(\text{event})=\frac{\text{number of favourable outcomes}}{\text{total number of outcomes}}$$

Every probability lies on a scale from $0$ (impossible) to $1$ (certain). $\frac12$ means an even chance.

“A or B” means A happens, or B happens, or both. List the outcomes and count each one only once: $$P(A\text{ or }B)=P(A)+P(B)-P(A\text{ and }B)$$
Trap: adding $P(\text{odd})+P(\text{shaded})$ counts sector 3 twice, because it is both odd and shaded.
Play · click sectors to shade them, then choose an event
Worked example · Paper Question 6 (spinner 1–8, sectors 2, 3, 6 shaded)
  1. $P(5)=\dfrac18$, because one sector of eight shows 5.
  2. $P(\text{shaded})=\dfrac38$, from sectors 2, 3 and 6.
  3. Odd or shaded: $1,2,3,5,6,7$, which is six sectors, with 3 counted once. So $P=\dfrac68=\dfrac34$.
  4. Check with the rule: $\frac48+\frac38-\frac18=\frac68$.
  5. On the scale: landing on 9 is impossible ($0$). Landing on a number at most 8 is certain ($1$).
Proof of the “or” rule, with examples

Proof by counting. Say $a$ outcomes are in A, $b$ are in B, and $c$ are in both, out of $n$ equally likely outcomes. Counting A and then B counts the $c$ shared outcomes twice. So the number of outcomes in “A or B” is $a+b-c$, and $$P(A\text{ or }B)=\frac{a+b-c}{n}=\frac an+\frac bn-\frac cn.$$

Example 1. Cards 1–10, “even or multiple of 3”: $\frac{5}{10}+\frac{3}{10}-\frac{1}{10}=\frac{7}{10}$. Only 6 is in both.

Example 2. If A and B can't both happen (rolling a 1 or a 6 on one die), then $c=0$ and you just add: $\frac16+\frac16=\frac13$.

Example 3. “Not A” is everything else, so $P(\text{not }A)=1-P(A)$.

Station 7 · Probability

Experimental probability and expected counts

The idea

When we can't count equally likely outcomes (a robot, a basketball shot, a drawing pin), we estimate a probability from trials:

$$P(\text{event})\approx\frac{\text{times it happened}}{\text{number of trials}}$$

This fraction is called the relative frequency.

Expected number in $n$ more trials $=P(\text{event})\times n$. It is a best guess. Random variation means the real count will usually be a little above or below it.
Trap: more trials give a more reliable estimate. A small experiment can easily be far from the true probability.
Play · run the robot

The robot's true chance of a direct hit is set by the slider. In real life nobody knows the true chance; we only see the results.

Worked example · Paper Question 7 (robot, 80 tries)
  1. Wins (direct hits) $=48$, so $P(\text{win})\approx\dfrac{48}{80}=\dfrac35=0.6$.
  2. Not winning $=12+20=32$, so $P(\text{not win})\approx\dfrac{32}{80}=\dfrac25$. Check: $\frac35+\frac25=1$.
  3. Expected wins in $150$ more tries: $\dfrac35\times150=90$.
  4. It need not be exactly $90$. That is an estimate, and random variation means the result could be above or below it.
Why is “probability × trials” the expected number?

If a win happens with probability $p$, then in the long run it happens in a fraction $p$ of all tries. In $n$ tries, a fraction $p$ of $n$ is $p\times n$.

Example. A fair coin in 50 tosses: we expect $\frac12\times50=25$ heads. Getting 23 or 28 is normal.

Example. A fair die rolled 300 times: we expect $\frac16\times300=50$ sixes.

Station 8 · Probability

Sample spaces: two dice

The idea

A sample space lists every possible outcome. Two fair dice give $6\times6=36$ ordered pairs, each equally likely. A table shows them all, so we can count:

$$P(\text{event})=\frac{\text{cells that fit}}{36}$$

$(2,6)$ and $(6,2)$ are different cells, because the first die and the second die are different dice. $(4,4)$ is only one cell.
Trap: for “at least one die shows 5”, there are 6 cells with 5 on the first die and 6 with 5 on the second, but $(5,5)$ is in both. So the count is $6+6-1=11$, not 12.
Play · choose an event and see its cells

Theory and experiment

Bars show the experiment. Outlines show what the fair-dice model expects.
Worked example · Paper Question 8
  1. Sum of 8: $(2,6),(3,5),(4,4),(5,3),(6,2)$, so $P=\dfrac{5}{36}$.
  2. Sum less than 5 (sums 2, 3, 4): $1+2+3=6$ cells, so $P=\dfrac{6}{36}=\dfrac16$.
  3. Game A (7 or 11): $6+2=8$ cells, so $\dfrac{8}{36}=\dfrac29$. Game B (at least one 5): $6+6-1=11$ cells, so $\dfrac{11}{36}$. Game B is more likely to win.
  4. Experiment: 9 sums of 8 in 36 rolls gives $\dfrac{9}{36}=\dfrac14$. The model predicts $\dfrac{5}{36}\times36=5$.
  5. This does not prove the dice are biased. 36 rolls is a small experiment. Record each die separately over many more rolls and compare with $\frac16$ for each face.

Station 9 · Probability

Tree diagrams: picking without replacement

The idea

A tree shows events in stages. Each branch carries a probability, and the branches from one point add to $1$.

Multiply along a path to get the probability of that whole path. Add down the paths when more than one path fits the event.

Without replacement, the first item is not put back. The second draw has one fewer item, and its probabilities depend on what came out first.

Trap: “exactly one cinema pass” has two orders, cinema-then-pool and pool-then-cinema. Include both paths.
Play · change the tickets
Cinema (C): Pool (P):
Worked example · Paper Question 9 (5 cinema, 7 pool, two drawn)
  1. First draw: $P(C)=\dfrac{5}{12}$, $P(P)=\dfrac{7}{12}$.
  2. After a C is removed, 11 tickets remain: $\dfrac{4}{11}$ C and $\dfrac{7}{11}$ P. After a P is removed: $\dfrac{5}{11}$ C and $\dfrac{6}{11}$ P.
  3. $P(\text{both C})=\dfrac{5}{12}\times\dfrac{4}{11}=\dfrac{20}{132}=\dfrac{5}{33}$.
  4. $P(\text{exactly one C})=\dfrac{5}{12}\times\dfrac{7}{11}+\dfrac{7}{12}\times\dfrac{5}{11}=\dfrac{70}{132}=\dfrac{35}{66}$.
  5. The second-draw probabilities depend on the first ticket, because that ticket is gone and the mix of the remaining 11 has changed.
Why do we multiply along a branch?

“C then C” happens in $\frac{4}{11}$ of the cases where the first ticket was C. Those cases are $\frac{5}{12}$ of all cases. A fraction of a fraction is a product: $\frac{4}{11}$ of $\frac{5}{12}$ is $\frac{5}{12}\times\frac{4}{11}$.

Check by counting. There are $12\times11=132$ ordered ways to draw two tickets, and $5\times4=20$ of them are C then C. $\frac{20}{132}=\frac{5}{33}$. ✓

Shortcut. $P(\text{at least one C})=1-P(\text{no C})=1-\frac{7}{12}\times\frac{6}{11}=1-\frac{42}{132}=\frac{15}{22}$.

Station 10 · Probability

Two-way tables and “given that”

The idea

A two-way table sorts every person by two features at once. Each person is in exactly one row and one column. The totals check the table.

“A and B”: use the cell, out of the grand total.
“A given B”: only look at group B. Use the cell, out of the B total: $$P(A\mid B)=\frac{\text{number in A and B}}{\text{number in B}}$$
Trap: the word after “given that” chooses the denominator. $P(\text{walks}\mid\text{Year 10})$ and $P(\text{Year 10}\mid\text{walks})$ have different denominators.
Play · click a header for “given”, then click a cell

Click a row or column header to restrict the group (click it again to remove it). Then click a cell.

Worked example · Paper Question 10 (48 students)
  1. Missing entries: $18-8-4=6$, $18-5-10=3$, $12-6-4=2$. The last column total is $4+3+4=11$.
  2. $P(\text{one instrument and two languages})=\dfrac{10}{48}=\dfrac{5}{24}$.
  3. Given two or more instruments: only those 12 students count. $P(\text{one language})=\dfrac{2}{12}=\dfrac16$.
  4. Given two languages: only those 22 students count. $10+6=16$ of them play at least one instrument, so $P=\dfrac{16}{22}=\dfrac{8}{11}$.
