0 · Foundations

This lesson is about independence, chains of reasoning and simulations. They all rest on three basic tools. Work through them here, with a worked example and a widget for each, then check yourself.

“70% of students take the bus. Of these, 40% are late at least once a week.”

What fraction of all students are bus users who are late? Can you say what fraction of the late students take the bus? And if a survey of 20 students finds 9 late ones, is that the “true” probability? By the end of this tab you will know which of these you can answer, and why.

TOOL 1

The complement and addition rules

The idea
  • Complement: \(P(A')=1-P(A)\). “Not A” is everything outside A.
  • Addition rule: \(P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cap B)\). The overlap is counted twice when you add \(P(A)\) and \(P(B)\), so take it away once.
  • Mutually exclusive: no overlap, \(P(A\cap B)=0\), so \(P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)\).
  • Independent: \(P(A\cap B)=P(A)\times P(B)\). A different idea from mutually exclusive.

Widget · two-event Venn
\(P(A)\) \(P(B)\) \(P(A\cap B)\)
Remember

Draw the Venn diagram and fill the overlap first. Then every other region is a subtraction, and “neither” is \(1-P(A\cup B)\).

TOOL 2

“Of these…”: conditional probability and multiplying

The idea

“Of these bus students, 40% are late” is a conditional probability: \(P(\text{late}\mid\text{bus})=0.4\). It is not \(P(\text{late})\).

From the definition \(P(B\mid A)=\dfrac{P(A\cap B)}{P(A)}\) we get the multiplication rule \[P(A\cap B)=P(A)\times P(B\mid A)\] This is always true: it is multiplying along a tree branch. Using \(P(A)\times P(B)\) instead is only allowed for independent events.

Let's do it together
Remember

Write the given information in symbols first: “of the bus students” → \(\mid\text{bus}\). Then check which direction the question asks for.

TOOL 3

True, model and experimental probability

The idea
  • Model (theoretical) probability: worked out from an assumption, e.g. “the coin is fair, so \(P(\text{head})=0.5\)”.
  • Experimental probability: from trials, e.g. 62 heads in 100 flips gives \(0.62\). It changes from one experiment to the next.
  • True probability: the real long-run chance for this coin. We can never know it exactly.

The more trials, the closer the experimental probability usually gets to the true one (the law of large numbers). It is never guaranteed to be equal.

Remember

An experimental probability is an estimate of the true probability. Small samples can be far off; large samples are more reliable. This is the idea behind the simulation lab (Tab 2).

1 · Independence & bounds

Tool D · about 15 minutes

Technique

Three equivalent tests for independence. Choose the one that matches the information you are given.

  1. Conditional vs conditional: if \(P(B\mid A)=P(B\mid A')\), the events are independent. Use this when the question gives percentages within groups, such as “of shoppers with a regular day, 41.2%…”. You can decide without any further calculation (2016 Question 1(b)).
  2. Conditional vs overall: if \(P(B\mid A)=P(B)\), the events are independent.
  3. Product rule: if \(P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B)\), the events are independent. Use this when you are given a two-way table or joint and marginal probabilities.
  4. Mutually exclusive is a different idea: the events cannot happen together, so \(P(A\cap B)=0\). To show events are not mutually exclusive, find a non-zero joint probability.
  5. Excellence sentence: say what the comparison means in context, for example “knowing a student takes the bus does not change the probability that they are late”.
Worked example

The 2016 style. For shoppers with a regular shopping day, 41.2% shop at the weekend. For shoppers without one, 28.9% shop at the weekend. Are the events independent?

Excellence answer: “The events are not independent, because the probability of shopping at the weekend depends on whether the shopper has a regular shopping day: \(P(W\mid R)=0.412\ne P(W\mid R')=0.289\). Shoppers with a regular day are more likely to shop at the weekend.”

The 2015 'bounds' style (a chain of reasoning). 63.9% of imported cars are Japanese, and of these Japanese cars, 80.3% are used. Explain, from this information alone, why a used car is more likely to be Japanese than not.

Step 1 · What we know and what we want. \(P(J)=0.639\) and \(P(U\mid J)=0.803\). We want to show \(P(J\mid U)\gt 0.5\). We are not told how many non-Japanese cars are used, so we cannot calculate \(P(U)\) or \(P(J\mid U)\) exactly. Instead we find a bound.

Step 2 · Japanese and used. \(P(J\cap U)=0.639\times0.803=0.513\). This is 51.3% of all imported cars (not a share of the used cars).

Why can we multiply? “Of these cars manufactured in Japan, 80.3% were used” is a conditional probability: \(P(U\mid J)=0.803\), not \(P(U)\). Rearranging the conditional probability formula gives the multiplication: \[P(U\mid J)=\frac{P(J\cap U)}{P(J)}\;\Longrightarrow\;P(J\cap U)=P(J)\times P(U\mid J)=0.639\times0.803=0.513\] This is the same as multiplying along the branches of a tree diagram, and it does not need independence.
  • \(P(J)\times P(U\mid J)\): always true (it is the conditional probability formula).
  • \(P(J)\times P(U)\): only true if \(J\) and \(U\) are independent, and here we are not even given \(P(U)\).

Why not use the formula directly for \(P(J\mid U)\)? \(P(J\mid U)=\dfrac{P(J\cap U)}{P(U)}\). The numerator is 0.513, but the denominator \(P(U)=P(J\cap U)+P(J'\cap U)=0.513+\,?\) is unknown, because we are not told how many non-Japanese cars are used. That is exactly why we need a bound (Steps 3 and 4).

Watch the direction: we are given \(P(U\mid J)\) (given Japanese, is it used?) but asked about \(P(J\mid U)\) (given used, is it Japanese?).

Step 3 · Non-Japanese and used (largest possible). These cars are part of the non-Japanese cars, so \(P(J'\cap U)\le P(J')=1-0.639=0.361\). The largest value happens if every non-Japanese car is used.

Step 4 · Compare the two parts of the used cars. Every used car is either Japanese or not: \(P(U)=P(J\cap U)+P(J'\cap U)\). The Japanese part (0.513) is bigger than the other part (at most 0.361), so it is more than half of the used cars.

Conclusion: \(P(J\mid U)=\dfrac{0.513}{0.513+P(J'\cap U)}\ge\dfrac{0.513}{0.513+0.361}\approx0.587\gt 0.5\), whatever the number of non-Japanese used cars is.

A shorter chain from the 2015 schedule: \(P(J\cap U)=0.513\gt 0.5\), so \(P(J'\cap U)\le1-0.513=0.487\), which is smaller than 0.513.

Excellence answer: “\(P(\text{Japan}\cap\text{used})=0.639\times0.803=0.513\). The non-Japanese cars make up only 0.361 of all imported cars, so \(P(\text{not Japan}\cap\text{used})\) can be at most 0.361. So more of the used cars are Japanese than not, which means a used car is more likely to have been made in Japan.”

Common errors: (1) “80.3% is more than 50%, so it is Japanese”: 80.3% is \(P(U\mid J)\), which is the wrong direction. (2) Calling 0.513 “the share of used cars that are Japanese”: 0.513 is a share of all cars.

Theorem

Theorem 1 (equivalent tests). If \(0\lt P(A)\lt 1\), then the following are all equivalent: \[P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B)\iff P(B\mid A)=P(B)\iff P(B\mid A)=P(B\mid A')\]

Theorem 2. If \(A\) and \(B\) are mutually exclusive and both have non-zero probability, they are not independent.

Theorem 3 (bounds). If \(P(A\cap B)\gt P(A')\), then \(P(A\mid B)\gt \tfrac12\).

What they say, with concrete examples:

  • Theorem 1: bus users and non-bus users are both 30% late, so lateness is independent of taking the bus. The product test gives the same answer.
  • Theorem 2: 'Works at Henry's' and 'works at Bob's' (you can't work at both): knowing a student works at Henry's makes \(P(\text{Bob's})=0\), so the events are dependent.
  • Theorem 3: cars \(0.513\gt 0.361\), so a used car is more likely Japanese. Hospital: \(0.544\gt 0.36\), so an overnight patient is more likely to have come through ED.
Proofs (for interested readers)

Theorem 1. \(P(B\mid A)=\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(A)}\), so \(P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B)\iff P(B\mid A)=P(B)\).

By the law of total probability, \(P(B)=P(A)P(B\mid A)+(1-P(A))P(B\mid A')\). This is a weighted average of the two conditional probabilities, so it equals \(P(B\mid A)\) exactly when \(P(B\mid A)=P(B\mid A')\).

Theorem 2. \(P(A\cap B)=0\), but \(P(A)P(B)\gt 0\), so the product rule fails.

Theorem 3. \(A'\cap B\) is part of \(A'\), so \(P(A'\cap B)\le P(A')\lt P(A\cap B)\). Since \(P(B)=P(A\cap B)+P(A'\cap B)\lt 2P(A\cap B)\), we get \(P(A\mid B)=\dfrac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)}\gt\dfrac12\). ∎

2 · Simulation lab

Tool E · about 15 minutes

The 5-step road for every simulation question: ① name the three probabilities → ② understand what the simulation shows → ③ count the results as extreme as the observed one → ④ decide “rare or not” and write the conclusion → ⑤ think about sample size.
Step 1 of 5 · Three kinds of probability

Look at one die. There are three different “probabilities of a 6”, and the exam expects you to name them correctly.

NameWhere it comes fromDie example
Model (theoretical)an assumption about the situation“the die is fair”, so \(P(6)=\tfrac16\)
Experimentaldata we collected13 sixes in 60 rolls: \(\tfrac{13}{60}\approx0.217\)
Truethe real long-run chance for this dieunknown: we can only estimate it

Remember: the experimental probability is an estimate of the true probability, and it changes from sample to sample. The model is only an assumption, and it might be wrong.

Check this step

Step 2 of 5 · What a simulation shows

The situation. A shop claims that 10% of its receipts are “lucky receipts” (model: \(p=0.1\)). Tama collected 25 receipts and 6 were lucky. Is that surprising?

The idea. If the claim is true, 25 receipts should give about \(25\times0.1=2.5\) lucky ones. But samples vary, so a real sample might give 1, 3 or 4 by chance alone. A simulation shows how much results vary by chance alone:

  1. Assume the model is true (\(p=0.1\)).
  2. Let a computer collect a sample of the same size as the real one (25 receipts) and count the lucky ones.
  3. Repeat many times (here 1000) and record each count.

The table below is the result. Each number tells you in how many of the 1000 simulated samples that count happened.

Lucky receipts in 25012345678+Total
Number of simulations721992662261386524731000

Read it like this: 266 of the 1000 samples had exactly 2 lucky receipts, so 2 is the most common result. Results from 1 to 4 are typical when the claim is true.

Step 3 of 5 · Count the results as extreme as the observed one

We now ask: if the claim is true, how often would we get a result at least as far from 2.5 as Tama’s?

  1. Expected value: \(np=25\times0.1=2.5\).
  2. Which direction? Tama’s 6 is above 2.5, so “as extreme or more” means 6 or more. (If the observed value were below the expected value, we would count that value or fewer.)
  3. Add up the matching columns: \(24+7+3=34\) simulations.
  4. Turn it into a proportion: \(\tfrac{34}{1000}=0.034\), about 3.4%.

Check this step

Step 4 of 5 · Decide “rare or not”, then write the conclusion

Rule of thumb: if the proportion from Step 3 is under about 5%, the result is rare under the model.

Rare (under 5%)Not rare
You CAN say“unlikely to happen by chance alone if the model is true”, so there is evidence against the model“consistent with the model”: chance alone could explain it
You CANNOT saywhat the cause is (“the shop is cheating”), or that the model is proven wrongthat the model is proven true

Two contrasting examples with the same table:

  • Tama, 6 lucky: 0.034 (3.4%) is rare, so the claim of 10% is doubtful.
  • Kiri, 4 lucky: “4 or more” \(=138+65+24+7+3=237\), so 0.237 (23.7%). This is not rare, so his result is consistent with the 10% claim.
Sentence frame: “If the [model] is true, a result as extreme as [observed] happened in only [x] of the [N] simulations, so it is (un)likely to occur by chance alone. This suggests …, but [limitation, such as sample size or factors not controlled].”

Check this step

Step 5 of 5 · Sample size: why more data is more convincing

In tool E, run Example 1 (30 games) and then Example 2 (300 games), both with \(p=0.25\). Compare the lower chart (experimental proportions on a fixed 0–1 scale) for the two runs. The upper chart rescales itself, so use the lower one.

  • With 30 games, proportions from about 0.10 to 0.40 are common: the histogram is wide.
  • With 300 games, they stay close to 0.25: the histogram is narrow.

So: a result far from the model is less surprising in a small sample and more convincing in a large one. The experimental probability gets closer to the true probability as \(n\) grows, but it is never guaranteed to equal it.

Check this step

Theorem

Going deeper (optional). Theorem (how much experimental proportions vary). If each of \(n\) independent trials has true probability \(p\), the experimental proportion \(\hat p\) has mean \(p\) and standard deviation \[\sigma_{\hat p}=\sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}}\] So as \(n\) grows, \(\hat p\) settles towards the true probability \(p\) (the law of large numbers).

What it says, with concrete examples:

  • Spinner: with \(p=0.25\) and \(n=30\), \(\sigma\approx0.079\), so proportions from about 0.10 to 0.40 are common. With \(n=300\), \(\sigma\approx0.025\). This is exactly what Step 5 showed.
  • Male shoppers (2016): with only 31 males, \(\sigma\approx0.09\), so a 45% estimate could easily be 9 percentage points away from the truth. This is why the examiners accept “small sample” reasons.
  • What it can do: to halve the spread, you need about 4 times as many trials.
Proof sketch (for interested readers)

Let \(X_i=1\) if trial \(i\) is a success, and 0 otherwise. Then \(E(X_i)=p\) and \(\text{Var}(X_i)=p-p^2=p(1-p)\).

For independent trials, variances add: \(\text{Var}(\sum X_i)=np(1-p)\).

\(\hat p=\frac1n\sum X_i\), so \(\text{Var}(\hat p)=\frac{np(1-p)}{n^2}=\frac{p(1-p)}{n}\). ∎

3 · Writing for Excellence

About 5 minutes, then the timed mini-paper (printed).

Technique

Turning a Merit answer into an Excellence answer. The marking schedules show that Excellence is usually earned in the sentences. Learn these six frames. In the exam, answers are written in English, so use these exact phrases.

1 · Relative risk: “[Group 1] were k times as likely as [group 2] to [outcome] (risk a compared with b).”
2 · Conditional probability in context: “Of the [given group], [x]% [outcome], so \(P(\text{outcome}\mid\text{given})=\dots\)”
3 · Independence: “The events are (not) independent because \(P(B\mid A)=\dots\) is (not) equal to \(P(B\mid A')=\dots\), so knowing [A] (does not change / changes) the probability that [B].”
4 · Assumption: “I assumed [independence / large population]. This is reasonable (or not) because [reason specific to the context].”
5 · Generalisation (two reasons): “The estimate comes from [one place / one time / n = …], so … First, … Second, …”
6 · Simulation: “If the [model] is true, a result as extreme as [observed] happened in only [x] of [N] simulations, so it is unlikely to occur by chance alone. This suggests …, but [limitation].”
Worked example

Question: a demonstrator approached 528 female and 31 male shoppers in one supermarket. 54.5% of the females and 45.2% of the males tasted the product. She claims females in general are more likely to taste it. Discuss.

Merit answer: “The sample might not be representative, so she should be careful.” This is one vague reason with no context.

Excellence answer: “Females were \(0.545/0.452=1.2\) times as likely to taste the product in this sample. However, first, only 31 males were approached, so the male estimate of 45.2% has a lot of sampling variability: the true proportion could easily be higher or lower. Second, the data comes from one supermarket and one demonstrator, so shoppers at other locations or times, or with a different demonstrator, may behave differently. So the claim can't be generalised to shoppers in general from this data.”
