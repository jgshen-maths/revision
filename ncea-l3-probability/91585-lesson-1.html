TOOL 1

Probability from a two-way table

The idea

Probability \(=\dfrac{\text{number in the event}}{\text{number in the group you are choosing from}}\). The only question is: which group?

  • “and” (\(\cap\)): one cell, out of everyone.
  • “or” (\(\cup\)): every cell in either event, counted once, out of everyone.
  • “given” (\(\mid\)): the group after the bar is the new “everyone”. Cover the rest of the table.

Widget · what is the denominator?

Pick an event. Orange cells are the numerator; the green outline is the group you divide by.

Remember

Find the word given, of those, if or out of the ___. That group is the denominator. \(P(A\mid B)\) and \(P(B\mid A)\) are different numbers.

TOOL 2

Tree diagrams and total probability

The idea
  1. Each set of branches from one point adds to 1.
  2. Multiply along a path to get “this and then that”.
  3. Add the paths that end in the event you want (total probability).
  4. Same thing with counts: imagine 100 (or 1000) days and split them.

\(P(\text{rain})\) \(P(\text{late}\mid\text{rain})\) \(P(\text{late}\mid\text{no rain})\)
Remember

Multiply along, add down. Check the ends add to 1. If the question then asks “given late, what is the chance it rained?”, divide the rain-and-late path by all the late paths: that is Tab 1.

TOOL 3

Independence (and the mutually exclusive trap)

The idea

Two events are independent if knowing one happened does not change the probability of the other: \(P(A\mid B)=P(A)\).

Then, and only then, \(P(A\cap B)=P(A)\times P(B)\). Either test works. With real data, “equal” means “close enough”, so say so.

Trap: mutually exclusive means the events cannot happen together: \(P(A\cap B)=0\). That is not independence. If one happens, you know the other didn't, so they are strongly dependent.

TOOL 4

Risk and relative risk

The idea

Risk is just a conditional probability: the proportion of a group that has the outcome. \(\text{risk}=\dfrac{\text{number in the group with the outcome}}{\text{group total}}\).

Relative risk compares two groups: \(\text{RR}=\dfrac{\text{risk in group 1}}{\text{risk in group 2}}\). RR \(=1\): same risk. RR \(>1\): group 1 is riskier. RR \(<1\): group 1 is safer.

Let's do it together
Remember

Say “___ times as likely”, name both groups and the outcome, in context. Avoid “times more likely”.

Your toolbox for the rest of the lesson
  1. Two-way table: the given group is the denominator. \(P(\text{tasted}\mid\text{female})=\tfrac{120}{200}=0.6\).
  2. Tree: multiply along, add the paths. \(0.2\times0.5+0.8\times0.1=0.18\).
  3. Reverse conditional: the target path divided by all the paths with that result. \(\tfrac{0.10}{0.18}=0.556\) (Tab 1).
  4. Independence: \(P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B)\) only when \(P(A\mid B)=P(A)\).
  5. Relative risk: \(\tfrac{0.06}{0.01}=6\): six times as likely.

1 · Reverse conditionals

Tool A · about 17 minutes

Technique

The frequency method for reverse conditional probability. Use it whenever a question gives \(P(\text{result}\mid\text{situation})\) but asks for \(P(\text{situation}\mid\text{result})\). Typical results are an alarm, a test or a red light.

  1. Imagine a convenient population, such as 10 000.
  2. Split it by the base rate: who really has the situation and who doesn't.
  3. Split each group by the result rates. You now have four counts: TP, FN, FP and TN.
  4. Answer the question: \(P(C\mid +)=\dfrac{TP}{TP+FP}\). The denominator is everyone with that result.
  5. Write the answer as a sentence in context, and comment on what it means. This last step is where the Excellence mark is.
Worked example

Bank fraud alerts. 0.5% of card transactions are fraudulent. The alert system flags 96% of fraudulent transactions, and it also flags 2% of genuine ones. A transaction is flagged. How likely is it to be fraud?

Take 10 000 transactions:

  • fraud: \(0.005\times10\,000=50\), of which \(0.96\times50=48\) are flagged (TP)
  • genuine: \(9950\), of which \(0.02\times9950=199\) are flagged (FP)

\[P(\text{fraud}\mid\text{flag})=\frac{48}{48+199}=\frac{48}{247}=0.194\]

Excellence sentence: “Only about 19.4% of flagged transactions are actually fraud. Because fraud is rare (0.5%), the 2% of genuine transactions that are flagged (199) far outnumber the true fraud alerts (48). So the bank should check a flagged transaction before blocking the card.”

Load this example in tool A to see it.

Theorem

Law of total probability: \(P(B)=P(A)P(B\mid A)+P(A')P(B\mid A')\).

Bayes' theorem: \[P(A\mid B)=\frac{P(A)\,P(B\mid A)}{P(A)P(B\mid A)+P(A')P(B\mid A')}\]

What it says, with concrete examples:

  • Medical test: with base rate 2%, sensitivity 90% and false-positive rate 5%, a positive result gives only \(P(C\mid+)\approx0.27\). The test is good, but the condition is rare.
  • Same test, higher base rate: if 30% of the people tested have the condition (for example, only people with symptoms are tested), \(P(C\mid+)=\frac{0.27}{0.27+0.035}=0.885\). Who is tested changes the meaning of a positive result.
  • Checkout machine (2016 exam): mismatch rate 4%, red light 98% for a mismatch and 3% for a match, so \(P(\text{mismatch}\mid\text{red})=\frac{0.0392}{0.068}=0.576\).
  • What it can do: it turns the probability a test-maker knows, \(P(+\mid C)\), into the one a patient or a manager needs, \(P(C\mid+)\).
Proof (for interested readers)

By the definition of conditional probability, \(P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B\mid A)\) and also \(P(A\cap B)=P(B)P(A\mid B)\).

Equate the two: \(P(A\mid B)=\dfrac{P(A)P(B\mid A)}{P(B)}\).

Next, \(B=(B\cap A)\cup(B\cap A')\), and these two pieces cannot both happen, so \(P(B)=P(B\cap A)+P(B\cap A')=P(A)P(B\mid A)+P(A')P(B\mid A')\). This is the law of total probability.

Substitute it into the denominator to get Bayes' theorem. ∎

2 · Three-set Venn

Tool B · about 15 minutes

Technique

Filling a three-set Venn diagram from clues: always work from the centre outward.

  1. Put the all three number in the centre.
  2. Each exactly two region = (pair total) − (centre).
  3. Each only region = (set total) − (the three other pieces of that circle).
  4. None = overall total − (everything inside the circles).
  5. Check: the 8 regions add to the total. Then answer using the regions. For a conditional probability, the given set is the denominator.

Watch the wording: “A and B” includes the centre. “A and B but not C” is the exactly-two region. “Only A” is the outer part of A. Exam clues often mix these (for example, the 2016 Question 2(c)).

Worked example

Rotorua tourists. 200 tourists were asked which of three attractions they visited: Hot pools (A), Te Puia (B) and the Redwoods (C).

  • 86 visited A, 74 visited B and 74 visited C.
  • 30 visited A and B, 22 visited A and C, and 26 visited B and C.
  • 12 visited all three.

Press Next step in tool B to fill the diagram one region at a time. Then choose events from the list.

For example, \(P(\text{none})=\tfrac{32}{200}=0.16\) and \(P(A\mid B)=\tfrac{30}{74}=0.405\).

Check with inclusion–exclusion: \(n(A\cup B\cup C)=86+74+74-30-22-26+12=168\), so none \(=200-168=32\) ✓

Theorem

Inclusion–exclusion for three sets: \[\begin{aligned}n(A\cup B\cup C)={}&n(A)+n(B)+n(C)\\&-n(A\cap B)-n(A\cap C)-n(B\cap C)\\&+n(A\cap B\cap C)\end{aligned}\]

What it says, with concrete examples:

  • Rotorua: \(86+74+74-30-22-26+12=168\) tourists visited at least one attraction.
  • Finding the centre: if you know the union and all the other totals, you can rearrange to find \(n(A\cap B\cap C)\). Exam clues sometimes hide the centre this way.
  • Probability form: divide by \(N\). For example, \(P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cap B)\). If \(A\) and \(B\) are mutually exclusive, the last term is 0.
Proof (for interested readers)

Count how many times a person in each region is counted by the right-hand side.

  • In exactly one set: \(+1\).
  • In exactly two sets: \(+2-1=1\).
  • In all three: \(+3-3+1=1\).
  • In none: 0.

So every person in the union is counted exactly once. ∎

3 · Combining distributions

Tool C · about 12 minutes

Technique

Combining two independent random variables. This is the 2015 'motorcycle at least two years older than the car' type of question.

  1. If the question has a missing probability, find it first: the probabilities in a distribution add to 1.
  2. Draw an X-by-Y grid. Each cell is \(P(X=x)\times P(Y=y)\). This uses independence, so state it.
  3. Mark the cells that satisfy the condition, such as \(X-Y\ge2\), and list them.
  4. Add them. Organise the sum by one variable, for example \(P(X=2)P(Y=0)+P(X=3)P(Y\le1)+\dots\)
  5. Comment on whether the independence assumption is reasonable in context.

Staged problems (like the 2016 voucher question): split by the first stage with the law of total probability, then use the second distribution inside each branch.

Worked example

Gym visits. \(X\) is the number of days Aroha goes to the gym in a week, and \(Y\) is the number for Ben. Their distributions are in tool C, and we assume they are independent. Find \(P(X\ge Y+2)\).

The cells with \(X-Y\ge2\) are (2,0), (3,0), (3,1), (4,0), (4,1) and (4,2):

\[\begin{aligned}&0.3(0.25)+0.25(0.25+0.3)\\&\quad+0.15(0.25+0.3+0.25)\\&=0.075+0.1375+0.12=0.3325\end{aligned}\]
Assumption sentence: “I assumed Aroha's and Ben's gym days are independent. This may not be reasonable if they train together, because then their numbers of days would be related.”
Theorem

Product rule for independent random variables: if \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent, then \(P(X=x\text{ and }Y=y)=P(X=x)\,P(Y=y)\), and for any event \(E\) about the pair, \[P(E)=\sum_{(x,y)\in E}P(X=x)P(Y=y)\]

What it says, with concrete examples:

  • Gym visits: \(P(X\ge Y+2)=0.3325\) (worked example).
  • A tie: \(P(X=Y)\) is the sum of the diagonal cells.
  • Vouchers (2016): \(P(\text{used in time})=\sum_d P(\text{delay}=d)\,P(U\le10-d)\). The same idea is applied in stages.
Why it works (for interested readers)

The pairs \((x,y)\) are mutually exclusive outcomes: two different cells cannot both happen. So the probability of an event is the sum of its cells (the addition rule).

Independence means each cell's probability is the product of the two marginal probabilities. ∎

4 · Exit ticket

5 minutes, with no help.

Technique

When the base rate is the unknown. Sometimes the question gives you \(P(+)\) and asks for the base rate. Write the total-probability equation with \(p\) as the unknown and solve it. This is the Excellence 'devise a strategy' step.

Worked example

A smoke alarm sounds on 6% of nights. It sounds on 95% of nights with real smoke and on 4% of nights without smoke. What proportion of nights have real smoke?

\[0.95p+0.04(1-p)=0.06\Rightarrow0.91p=0.02\Rightarrow p=0.022\]

Then \(P(\text{smoke}\mid\text{alarm})=\dfrac{0.95\times0.022}{0.06}=0.348\).
