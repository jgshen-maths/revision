0 · The five distributions

About 25 minutes. One distribution at a time: when to use it → the formula → a small example done together → the calculator → check yourself. Finish with the distribution explorer and the “which one?” game.

Five questions, five different shapes of randomness.
  • How many of the 12 eggs in a carton are cracked?
  • How many calls arrive at a helpline in 10 minutes?
  • How heavy is a kiwifruit?
  • When does the bus arrive between 8:00 and 8:20?
  • How much is a taxi fare, if it is at least $12, at most $60 and usually about $25?

Each one needs a different model. By the end of this tab you will know which, why, and how to calculate with it.

STEP 1 OF 6

Binomial

When to use it

Counting “successes” in a fixed number \(n\) of trials, where

  • each trial has two outcomes (success or failure),
  • the probability of success \(p\) is the same every time, and
  • the trials are independent.

Formula. If \(X\sim B(n,p)\), then for \(x=0,1,\dots,n\): \[P(X=x)=\binom{n}{x}p^{x}(1-p)^{n-x}\]\[\text{mean}=np,\qquad \text{SD}=\sqrt{np(1-p)}\]

What each part means. \(p^{x}\) = the probability of \(x\) successes; \((1-p)^{n-x}\) = the probability of the other \(n-x\) failures; \(\binom{n}{x}\) = the number of different orders the successes and failures can come in.

STEP 2 OF 6

Poisson

When to use it

Counting events in an interval of time or space (calls per hour, flaws per metre), where the events

  • happen at random and independently,
  • at a constant average rate \(\lambda\), and
  • one at a time (never two at exactly the same moment).

Unlike the binomial, there is no fixed maximum.

Formula. If \(X\sim\text{Po}(\lambda)\), then for \(x=0,1,2,\dots\): \[P(X=x)=\frac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^{x}}{x!}\]\[\text{mean}=\text{variance}=\lambda,\qquad \text{SD}=\sqrt\lambda\]

The rate scales with the interval. 2 per day = 14 per week = 1 per half-day. Always change \(\lambda\) to the interval in the question before calculating.

STEP 3 OF 6

Normal

When to use it

For continuous measurements (mass, time, length) whose distribution is symmetric and bell-shaped, described by a mean \(\mu\) and standard deviation \(\sigma\): \(X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2)\).

Probability = area under the curve. For a continuous variable \(P(X=a)=0\), so \(P(X\lt a)=P(X\le a)\). There is no simple formula for the area, so use the calculator (Ncd). The curve itself is \(f(x)=\frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-\frac12\left(\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\right)^2}\) (for reference only).

The 68–95–99.7 rule (a quick check on the calculator): about 68% of values lie within 1 SD of the mean, 95% within 2 SD and 99.7% within 3 SD.

STEP 4 OF 6

Uniform

When to use it

A continuous variable can take any value between a minimum \(a\) and a maximum \(b\), and every value is equally likely: \(X\sim U(a,b)\). The graph is a rectangle.

Formula. The total area must be 1 and the width is \(b-a\), so the height is \(\frac{1}{b-a}\): \[f(x)=\frac{1}{b-a}\;(a\le x\le b)\]\[P(c\lt X\lt d)=\frac{d-c}{b-a}\]\[\text{mean}=\frac{a+b}{2},\qquad \text{SD}=\frac{b-a}{\sqrt{12}}\]

Small example. A bus comes every 10 minutes, so the waiting time is \(X\sim U(0,10)\), with height \(\frac1{10}\).
  • \(P(2\lt X\lt 5)=\) width × height \(=3\times\frac1{10}=0.3\)
  • \(P(X\gt 8)=2\times\frac1{10}=0.2\)
  • Mean wait \(=\frac{0+10}{2}=5\) minutes

No calculator menu needed: it is just the area of a rectangle.

STEP 5 OF 6

Triangular

When to use it

A continuous variable with a known minimum \(a\), maximum \(b\) and most likely value (mode) \(c\): \(X\sim\text{Tri}(a,b,c)\). It can be skewed, which a normal model cannot.

Formula. The graph is a triangle with base \(b-a\). The area must be 1, so \(\tfrac12(b-a)\times h=1\) and the peak height is \(h=\dfrac{2}{b-a}\). The mean is \(\dfrac{a+b+c}{3}\).

To find a probability, cut at \(x\) and use the piece that is a single small triangle: area = ½ × base × height at the cut. Tab 1 goes through this step by step.

STEP 6 OF 6

Choosing the right distribution

Ask in this order
  1. Is it a count (0, 1, 2, …)? A fixed number of trials \(n\) → binomial; events in an interval with no upper limit → Poisson.
  2. Is it a measurement? Look at what the question gives you:
    • a mean and SD, symmetric and bell-shaped → normal
    • a minimum and maximum, every value equally likely → uniform
    • a minimum, a maximum and a most likely value → triangular.

1 · Areas and conditional probability

Tool A · about 15 minutes · simple models first, then exam-sized ones

Step 1 of 5 · Probability = area

For a continuous variable, the probability of an interval is the area under the graph over that interval, and the total area is 1. The uniform and triangular graphs are rectangles and triangles, so you can find the areas by hand.

Example. \(U(0,10)\): \(P(X\gt 8)=\) a rectangle of width 2 and height \(\frac1{10}\) \(=0.2\).

Check this step

Step 2 of 5 · The height of a triangle anywhere

Use the simple model \(X\sim\text{Tri}(0,10,4)\) (see the diagram).

  1. Peak height: \(h=\frac{2}{b-a}=\frac{2}{10}=0.2\), at the mode \(x=4\).
  2. Left of the mode, the height rises in a straight line from 0 (at \(a=0\)) to 0.2 (at \(c=4\)). \(x=2\) is exactly halfway, so \(h(2)=\frac{2}{4}\times0.2=0.1\).
    In general: \(h(x)=\dfrac{x-a}{c-a}\times h\).
  3. Right of the mode, the height falls from 0.2 (at \(c=4\)) to 0 (at \(b=10\)). At \(x=7\) there are 3 of the 6 units left to go to \(b\), so \(h(7)=\frac36\times0.2=0.1\).
    In general: \(h(x)=\dfrac{b-x}{b-c}\times h\).
Step 3 of 5 · Areas: left, right and in between
  1. Left of a point left of the mode (orange): a small triangle with base \(2-0=2\) and height \(h(2)=0.1\), so \(P(X\lt 2)=\tfrac12\times2\times0.1=0.1\).
  2. Right of a point right of the mode (green): a small triangle with base \(10-7=3\) and height \(h(7)=0.1\), so \(P(X\gt 7)=\tfrac12\times3\times0.1=0.15\).
  3. Between two points on either side of the mode: the middle region is not a triangle, so subtract: \(P(2\lt X\lt 7)=1-0.1-0.15=0.75\).

Shortcut formulas (the same calculation in one line): \[P(X\lt x)=\frac{(x-a)^2}{(b-a)(c-a)}\;\;(x\le c)\]\[P(X\gt x)=\frac{(b-x)^2}{(b-a)(b-c)}\;(x\ge c)\] Check: \(P(X\lt 2)=\frac{2^2}{10\times4}=0.1\) ✓. In the exam, write the height or the formula: the schedule rewards the working.

Now an exam-sized model. Garden-centre spending \(X\sim\text{Tri}(20,200,80)\): exactly the same three steps.

Check this step

Step 4 of 5 · Conditional probability in a model

“Given…” questions use \(P(A\mid B)=\dfrac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)}\). Follow these steps:

  1. Draw the given region \(B\) first: it is the new “everything”, the denominator.
  2. Find the overlap \(A\cap B\): the part of \(A\) inside \(B\), the numerator. If \(A\) lies completely inside \(B\), the overlap is just \(A\).
  3. Divide.
A simple example first. \(X\sim U(0,10)\). Find \(P(X\gt 8\mid X\gt 5)\).
  1. Given region: \(X\gt 5\), \(P(X\gt 5)=\frac{5}{10}=0.5\).
  2. The overlap of “\(X\gt 8\)” and “\(X\gt 5\)” is just \(X\gt 8\): \(P=\frac{2}{10}=0.2\).
  3. \(\frac{0.2}{0.5}=0.4\).

Meaning: after waiting 5 minutes already, 2 of the remaining 5 minutes count as “more than 8 minutes”.

The same steps on a triangle (a courier). Delivery time \(X\sim\text{Tri}(10,50,20)\), peak height \(\frac{2}{40}=0.05\). Find \(P(X\gt 30\mid X\gt 20)\).
  1. Given: \(P(X\gt 20)\) = the whole right triangle \(=\tfrac12\times30\times0.05=0.75\).
  2. Overlap = \(X\gt 30\): height \(h(30)=\frac{50-30}{50-20}\times0.05=0.0333\), area \(\tfrac12\times20\times0.0333=0.3333\).
  3. \(\frac{0.3333}{0.75}=0.4444\).

A normal model uses exactly the same steps, with each area from Ncd: \(P(X\gt 110\mid X\gt 100)=\dfrac{\text{Ncd}(110,\infty)}{\text{Ncd}(100,\infty)}\).

Step 5 of 5 · Is the model sensible?

A normal model always gives some probability to every value, including impossible ones (negative times or prices). Find \(P(X\lt 0)\): if it is not tiny, point out the problem in context and suggest a model with a fixed minimum (uniform or triangular).

Example. Waiting time \(N(6,4^2)\): \(P(X\lt 0)=0.0668\), so about 1 wait in 15 would be negative. The normal model is not suitable.

Check this step

Theorem

Theorem (triangular areas). For \(\text{Tri}(a,b,c)\): the peak height is \(h=\frac{2}{b-a}\), and for \(a\le x\le c\), \(P(X\lt x)=\dfrac{(x-a)^2}{(b-a)(c-a)}\). The right side works the same way.

What it says, with concrete examples:

  • \(\text{Tri}(0,250,130)\): \(h=0.008\), \(P(X\gt 150)=\frac{100^2}{250\times120}=\tfrac13\) (2022 exam).
  • \(\text{Tri}(0.1,0.35,0.16)\): \(P(X\lt 0.16)=\frac{0.06^2}{0.25\times0.06}=0.24\), which then becomes the binomial \(p\) (2023).
  • What it can do: find any probability for a bounded, skewed variable without a calculator menu.
Proof (for interested readers)

The total area is 1: \(\tfrac12(b-a)h=1\Rightarrow h=\frac{2}{b-a}\).

On \([a,c]\), the left triangle up to \(x\) is similar to the whole left triangle (base \(c-a\), height \(h\), area \(\tfrac12(c-a)h=\frac{c-a}{b-a}\)). Areas of similar triangles are in the ratio of the squares of their sides, so \[P(X\lt x)=\frac{c-a}{b-a}\left(\frac{x-a}{c-a}\right)^2=\frac{(x-a)^2}{(b-a)(c-a)}.\] The right side is the same with \(b-x\) and \(b-c\). ∎

2 · Inverse normal and inverse Poisson

Tool B · about 15 minutes · six steps from z-values to constraints

Step 1 of 6 · What a z-value means

A z-value says how many standard deviations a value is from the mean: \[z=\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x=\mu+z\sigma\]

Simple example. Test scores \(X\sim N(50,10^2)\).
  • A score of 65: \(z=\frac{65-50}{10}=1.5\), so 1.5 SD above the mean.
  • A score of 35: \(z=\frac{35-50}{10}=-1.5\), so 1.5 SD below the mean.
  • \(z=2\) means \(x=50+2\times10=70\).

With z-values, every normal distribution becomes the standard normal \(Z\sim N(0,1)\). So when μ or σ is unknown, the calculator always uses the same setting (σ = 1, μ = 0).

Check this step

Step 2 of 6 · From a probability to z (InvN)

InvN is the reverse of Ncd: put in an area, get out a value.

  1. Sketch and mark the area the question describes.
  2. Choose the tail: area on the left (“less than”, “lowest 10%”) → Left; on the right (“more than”, “top 10%”) → Right.
  3. Enter the area with σ = 1, μ = 0 to get \(z\).
Examples.
  • “10% are above”: Tail Right, Area 0.1 → \(z=1.2816\) (positive, above the mean).
  • “5% are below”: Tail Left, Area 0.05 → \(z=-1.6449\) (negative, below the mean).

If μ and σ are both known and you only want \(x\), you can put the real μ and σ straight into InvN. The z method is only essential when μ or σ is unknown.

Step 3 of 6 · Finding a value, σ or μ

Use \(x=\mu+z\sigma\) and solve for the unknown. Find \(z\) first.

  • Finding \(x\). The top 10% of \(N(50,10^2)\): \(x=50+1.2816\times10=62.8\).
  • Finding \(\sigma\). \(N(50,\sigma^2)\) with 10% above 58: \(58=50+1.2816\sigma\), so \(\sigma=\frac{8}{1.2816}=6.24\).
  • Finding \(\mu\). \(N(\mu,3^2)\) with 5% below 20: \(20=\mu-1.6449\times3\), so \(\mu=24.93\).

Check this step

Step 4 of 6 · Two unknowns: μ and σ

Two conditions → two equations. Subtract to remove \(\mu\).

Example. 20% are below 40 and 10% are above 70.
  1. “20% below” gives \(z=-0.8416\); “10% above” gives \(z=1.2816\).
  2. \(\mu-0.8416\sigma=40\) and \(\mu+1.2816\sigma=70\).
  3. Subtract: \(2.1232\sigma=30\), so \(\sigma=14.13\).
  4. Substitute back: \(\mu=40+0.8416\times14.13=51.89\).

Check this step

Step 5 of 6 · Requirements on σ (constraints)

“No more than 0.5% above 80” means the area above 80 is at most 0.005. A smaller σ makes the curve narrower and the tails smaller, so each requirement gives an upper bound for σ.

  1. For each requirement, find \(z\) and solve \(z=\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\) for the bound on σ.
  2. σ must satisfy all the requirements at once, so take the smallest bound.
  3. Check both requirements with Ncd.

The worked example (coffee) and tool B show this: drag σ and watch each requirement change from ✓ to ✗.

Worked example

Café coffee temperatures. The temperature is \(N(68,\sigma^2)\) °C. Health rule: fewer than 0.5% of cups above 80 °C. Quality rule: fewer than 5% below 60 °C. What is the largest σ can be?

  • Health: \(\frac{80-68}{\sigma}\gt z_{0.995}=2.5758\Rightarrow\sigma\lt 4.6587\)
  • Quality: \(\frac{60-68}{\sigma}\lt z_{0.05}=-1.6449\Rightarrow\sigma\lt 4.8636\)

Both must hold → \(\sigma\lt 4.6587\) (the health rule is the one that decides).

Check with \(\sigma=4.65\): \(P(X\gt 80)=0.0050\) and \(P(X\lt 60)=0.0428\) ✓. These are tool B's starting settings.

Step 6 of 6 · Inverse Poisson, rates and the most likely value
  1. \(\lambda\) from \(P(X=0)\). \(P(X=0)=e^{-\lambda}\), so \(\lambda=-\ln P(X=0)\).
    Quick check: if \(\lambda=2\), \(P(0)=e^{-2}=0.1353\), and \(-\ln0.1353=2\) ✓.
  2. Scale the rate to the interval in the question: 2.4 per hour → \(2.4\times\frac{15}{60}=0.6\) per 15 minutes.
  3. The most likely value (the mode): compare neighbouring probabilities with Ppd. It is the whole-number part of \(\lambda\) (when \(\lambda\) is not a whole number). Example: \(\lambda=2.6\) gives \(P(1)=0.1931\), \(P(2)=0.2510\), \(P(3)=0.2176\), so the mode is 2.

Check this step

Theorem

Theorem (standardising). If \(X\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2)\), then \(Z=\frac{X-\mu}{\sigma}\sim N(0,1)\), so \(P(X\lt x)=p\iff x=\mu+z_p\sigma\).

Theorem (the Poisson zero term). If \(X\sim\text{Po}(\lambda)\), then \(P(X=0)=e^{-\lambda}\), so \(\lambda=-\ln P(X=0)\).

What they say, with concrete examples:

  • \(P(X\gt 58)=0.1\) with \(\mu=50\) → \(\sigma=\frac{8}{1.2816}=6.24\).
  • 2022: \(P(X=0)=0.01\) → \(\lambda=4.6\). 2025: \(P(X=0)=0.03\) → \(\lambda=3.5\), which is then compared with 2.1 to decide which system is more accurate.
Proof (for interested readers)

Standardising. \(P(X\lt x)=P\big(\frac{X-\mu}{\sigma}\lt \frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\big)=P(Z\lt z)\). A linear change of a normal variable is still normal, here with mean 0 and SD 1. So \(z_p=\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\), that is \(x=\mu+z_p\sigma\).

The zero term. \(P(X=k)=\frac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^k}{k!}\); put \(k=0\): \(\lambda^0=0!=1\). ∎

3 · Chaining distributions

About 16 minutes

Technique

Chaining distributions. Many Excellence questions link two distributions:

  1. Stage 1: use model 1 to find the probability \(p\) for one individual (one lawn, one hour, one sheep).
  2. Stage 2: a fixed number of independent individuals → \(Y\sim B(n,p)\); all of k → \(p^k\); two different people → multiply their two probabilities.
  3. Write both distributions with their parameters, for example “\(Y\sim B(8,0.1481)\)”.
  4. State the independence assumption in context, and judge whether it is reasonable (2022 Q1(iv), 2023 Q1(iv)).
Worked example

The 2023 fertiliser chain. Fertiliser use \(X\sim\text{Tri}(0.1,0.35,0.16)\) (tonnes per hectare). Find the probability that more than half of 10 farms use less than 0.16 t/ha.

Stage 1: \(p=P(X\lt 0.16)=\tfrac12\times0.06\times8=0.24\).

Stage 2: \(Y\sim B(10,0.24)\), \(P(Y\gt 5)=1-P(Y\le5)=0.0161\).

Assumption: “This assumes the farms decide independently. This may not hold if neighbouring farms use the same contractor or follow the same advice.”
