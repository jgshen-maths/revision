III Density

There is always room

Pick any two fractions. The mediant sits between them, and so on for ever.

Take [[1|3]] and [[1|2]]. Add the tops and add the bottoms and you get [[2|5]], which is bigger than [[1|3]] and smaller than [[1|2]]. This odd addition is called the mediant, and it always lands strictly between two fractions whose denominators are positive.

Start with [[0|1]] and [[1|1]], the two ends of the line. Take their mediant, then the mediants of the new neighbours, and keep going. Every fraction between 0 and 1 will turn up, each one exactly once and already in lowest terms. The whole family is arranged in a tree, called the Stern–Brocot tree after the German mathematician Moritz Stern (1858) and the French clockmaker Achille Brocot (1861), who used it to choose gear ratios.

Why it matters. Because there is always another fraction between any two, mathematicians say the rationals are dense. You can never find "the next fraction after [[1|3]]". There is no such thing.

Density sounds like completeness, but wait for the next panel. The rationals are packed so tightly that you cannot slip a pin between them, and still they have holes.

IV Egyptian fractions

The scribe who split everything

Around 1550 BCE, one scribe wrote a handbook of how to share loaves.

The Rhind Papyrus, now in the British Museum, was copied by a scribe named Ahmes from an older text. It opens with a table that writes [[2|n]] for every odd n from 3 to 101 as a sum of different unit fractions, fractions with 1 on top. For example [[2|7]] = [[1|4]] + [[1|28]].

Why would anyone do this? Imagine sharing 2 loaves among 7 workers. Cut each loaf into quarters and give everyone a quarter; one quarter remains, and you cut that into 7 slivers. Everyone gets exactly [[1|4]] and [[1|28]], and nobody has to argue about which piece is bigger.

The Eye of Horus. Egyptian legend writes the parts of the eye as [[1|2]], [[1|4]], [[1|8]], [[1|16]], [[1|32]] and [[1|64]]. They add to [[63|64]], and the story says the missing [[1|64]] is supplied by the god Thoth. It is the first picture of a series that gets closer to 1 without arriving.

In 1202 Fibonacci wrote a greedy recipe: at each step take the biggest unit fraction that fits, subtract, and repeat. It always finishes. Nobody knows, however, whether it always finds the shortest answer. Many questions about these sums are still open (see panel XI).

V A number that is not a ratio

The number that broke the school

A legend of the Pythagoreans, who believed that all is number, and number is ratio.

A square has sides of length 1. How long is its diagonal? By Pythagoras's own theorem, the diagonal squared is 1 + 1 = 2, so the diagonal is a number whose square is 2.

Ancient Babylonian scribes already knew a very good answer: a clay tablet from about 1800 BCE (YBC 7289) gives the diagonal as 1;24,51,10 in base 60, which is 1.41421296…, matching √2 to six significant figures. But is there a fraction p/q whose square is exactly 2? The Greeks proved that there is not.

The story, and it is a story, since historians cannot be certain, says the proof was discovered by Hippasus of Metapontum, and that the brotherhood was so shaken that he was drowned at sea. What we do know is that this was one of the great shocks in the history of thought: the fractions were not enough.

The proof that √2 is not a fraction

Suppose √2 = p/q in lowest terms. Then p2 = 2q2, so p2 is even, so p is even, say p = 2m. Then 4m2 = 2q2, so q2 = 2m2, so q is even too. But now p and q are both even, which contradicts "lowest terms". So no such fraction exists.

You met the ingredients in Chapter 1: even and odd are remainders mod 2, and the square of an odd number is odd.

VI Cantor's surprise

More fractions than whole numbers? No.

The strangest fact about the rationals is how few of them there are.

Between 0 and 1 there are infinitely many fractions, and they are dense. Surely there are far more fractions than whole numbers 1, 2, 3, …? In 1874 Georg Cantor showed the opposite: you can line up every positive fraction in a single list, with a first, a second, a third, and so on, and no fraction is ever missed.

The trick is to put the fractions [[n|d]] in a grid and walk along the diagonals, skipping any fraction already met in lowest terms. Press play and watch the walk.

The twist. Cantor then proved (in 1874, and again with his famous diagonal argument in 1891) that the decimals between 0 and 1 cannot be listed. For any list you write down, you can build a new decimal by changing the nth digit of the nth number, and the new number is not on the list. So the real numbers form a bigger infinity than the rationals.

If you could pick a real number at random, the chance that it is a fraction is zero. Fractions are everywhere on the line, and still almost all of the line lies elsewhere. That is the real reason for the next chapters of mathematics: to understand all the numbers that are not fractions.

VII Why decimals repeat

Ten turns of a wheel

The block 142857 is not a coincidence. It is a ring of six remainders.

In long division by 7 every step multiplies the remainder by 10 and reduces it modulo 7. Start at 1 and you visit 1, 3, 2, 6, 4, 5, and then you are back at 1. It is a clock with six positions, and the digits of [[1|7]] are written down as you go round it. You can start the clock at any hour: [[3|7]] starts at 3, and its digits are the same wheel read from another place. That is why 142857 × 3 = 428571.

For 13 there are two separate wheels, each with six remainders. Fractions [[1|13]], [[3|13]], [[4|13]], [[9|13]], [[10|13]] and [[12|13]] ride one wheel and are rotations of each other; [[2|13]], [[5|13]], [[6|13]], [[7|13]], [[8|13]] and [[11|13]] ride the other.

The length of every wheel is the same number, the period. When the period equals b − 1, as it does for 7, 17, 19, 23, 29, 47 and 59, there is just one wheel, and the prime is called a full-reptend prime. In 1801 the 24-year-old Carl Friedrich Gauss included the study of these decimal periods in his book Disquisitiones Arithmeticae, the same book that founded modular arithmetic, which you met in Chapter 1.

Open door. Are there infinitely many full-reptend primes? Emil Artin guessed in 1927 that about 37.4% of all primes are, and it is still unproved.

VIII Music

The sound of fractions

Two strings sound sweet together when their lengths are in a simple ratio.

The Pythagoreans noticed that halving a string raises the note by an octave (ratio 2:1), and that a ratio of 3:2 gives a perfect fifth and 4:3 a fourth. Simple fractions made pure sounds, and this convinced them that harmony was number.

Press the buttons and look at the wave. For simple ratios the two tones fall into step after a few beats and the picture repeats. For the ratio √2 : 1, which is not a fraction, the wave never repeats, and the interval sounds restless. That is the tritone, which medieval musicians nicknamed the "devil in music".

The comma that does not close. Climb twelve perfect fifths and you should land on the same note as seven octaves higher. But ([[3|2]])12 = [[531441|4096]] ≈ 129.746, while 27 = 128. The gap, the Pythagorean comma, is a ratio of [[531441|524288]], about a quarter of a semitone. Why can this never close? Because 312 is odd and 219 is even, and, as you saw in Chapter 1, prime factorisations are unique. No power of 3 ever equals a power of 2.

The cure that musicians finally adopted is to split the octave into 12 equal steps, each a ratio of 21/12 ≈ 1.05946, an irrational number. Every interval is slightly out of tune, and every key is playable. Rational ratios also approximate it well: [[18|17]] ≈ 1.05882 was used by Renaissance lute makers to place frets.

IX Continued fractions

The secret staircase

There is a way to write any number that reveals its best fraction approximations.

Take [[355|113]]. Write it as 3 and a bit: 3 + [[16|113]]. Flip the bit: [[113|16]] = 7 + [[1|16]]. So

[[355|113]] = 3 + 17 + 116

This staircase of fractions inside fractions is a continued fraction, written [3; 7, 16]. You have already done the algorithm: it is exactly Euclid's HCF algorithm from Chapter 1, keeping the quotients. Every fraction has a staircase that ends. An irrational number has one that goes on for ever.

Stop the staircase early and you get a convergent, and convergents are the best possible fractions for their size. For π = [3; 7, 15, 1, 292, …] they are 3, [[22|7]], [[333|106]], [[355|113]]. That last fraction, accurate to six decimals, was found by the astronomer Zu Chongzhi in fifth-century China. The big number 292 is the reason it is so good: a huge step means the previous fraction is very close.

The golden ratio is [1; 1, 1, 1, …], the most "stairlike" irrational and therefore the hardest to approximate by fractions. Its convergents are the ratios of Fibonacci numbers.

Practical magic. The tropical year is about 365.24219 days. Its staircase gives the convergents [[1|4]], [[7|29]] and [[8|33]]: the Julian leap-year rule (1 in 4), and a 33-year cycle with 8 leap years, which is the basis of the Persian calendar's accuracy. The Gregorian rule ([[97|400]]) is a different compromise. Gear makers use convergents for the same reason: the fewest teeth that give the ratio they need.

X Computers and money

When a tenth is not a tenth

Your computer counts in base 2. Chapter 2.4 told you which fractions terminate. It depends on the base!

A fraction terminates in base 10 exactly when its denominator has no prime factors except 2 and 5, because those are the primes of 10. In base 2 the only allowed prime is 2. So [[1|10]], so friendly in decimal, is an endless repeating "fraction" in binary: 0.0001100110011…. A computer must cut it off, and the cut-off value is slightly wrong. Add two such cut-offs and the error shows: in most programming languages 0.1 + 0.2 gives 0.30000000000000004.

The Babylonians used base 60, and now we can see why: 60 = 22·3·5 means [[1|2]], [[1|3]], [[1|4]], [[1|5]] and [[1|6]] all terminate. Ten has only two such friends.

The index that melted. In January 1982 the Vancouver Stock Exchange began a new index at 1000.000. After each trade the computer recalculated it and truncated (chopped off) the result at three decimals instead of rounding it. Thousands of tiny losses piled up. By November 1983 the index showed about 525, while the true value was about 1098.9. Truncation always rounds down, so the error never cancels. This is the lesson of Tab 5 of Chapter 2.4 with real money attached.
A tenth of a second. On 25 February 1991 an American Patriot missile battery in Dhahran failed to intercept an incoming missile. The system had been running for about 100 hours, and its clock, which counted tenths of a second, stored 0.1 with a truncated binary expansion. The tiny error accumulated to about a third of a second, enough to look in the wrong part of the sky. Twenty-eight soldiers died. Engineers now take rounding as seriously as arithmetic.

XI The frontier

Questions nobody can answer

Some of them can be understood with only what you know now.

Can every 4/n be split into three unit fractions? The Erdős–Straus conjecture (1948) says that for every n ≥ 2 there are positive whole numbers x, y, z with [[4|n]] = [[1|x]] + [[1|y]] + [[1|z]]. Computers have checked every n up to about 1017, and nobody has a proof. Try it with the toy.

Is e + π irrational? Both e and π are known to be irrational (Lambert, 1761, for π). Yet nobody knows whether their sum is a fraction. The same is open for e×π. At most one of them can be rational, but we cannot say which.

Is Euler's constant γ irrational? It is defined as the limit of 1 + [[1|2]] + … + [[1|n]] − ln n. It is about 0.5772, and no one knows whether it is a fraction. In 1978 Roger Apéry stunned the world by proving that ζ(3) = 1 + [[1|8]] + [[1|27]] + [[1|64]] + … is irrational. The same question for ζ(5) is still open.

A great problem is one that a child can state and a lifetime cannot finish.

scribe, Egypt

Ahmes

He tells us he copied the work from an older one. He was a teacher in a way: the papyrus is a book of worked problems, much like this course.

teacher, Flanders & the Netherlands

Simon Stevin

An engineer and bookkeeper who argued that decimals were so useful that governments should adopt them for every measure. It took two centuries, but he was right.

professor, Germany

Richard Dedekind

He asked what a number is, and answered with a cut: split the rationals into a lower set and an upper set, and the gap between them is a real number, even √2.

mathematician, Germany

Georg Cantor

He compared infinities and was attacked by powerful colleagues for it. Today his ideas are the foundation of modern mathematics.

astronomer, China

Zu Chongzhi

In the fifth century he found [[355|113]] for π, a record that stood for almost a thousand years.

mathematician, India

Srinivasa Ramanujan

His notebooks are full of continued fractions, found by insight rather than proof, and many have since been proved to be true.
