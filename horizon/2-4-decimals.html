Step 1: Place Value and the Powers of Ten

A decimal is a fraction written in a special costume: its denominator is always a power of ten. In this tab you will see why the decimal point works, how to turn decimals into fractions, and why the rules for adding, multiplying and dividing decimals are exactly the fraction rules from 2.3.

1

Real life first: the 100 m sprint

Scene 1: a hundredth of a second

Two sprinters finish a $100$ m race. Mia's time is $11.43$ s and Leo's is $11.4$ s. Who won? Many people think $11.43$ is “bigger” because it has more digits, but the digits do not decide. Write both with the same number of places: $11.43$ and $11.40$. Now compare: $11.40<11.43$, so Leo was faster, by $\dfrac3{100}$ of a second.

What does the digit $3$ mean in $11.43$? It is in the second place after the point, so it counts hundredths: $3\times\dfrac1{100}$. Every place to the right of the decimal point is a power of ten in the denominator: $\dfrac1{10}$, $\dfrac1{100}$, $\dfrac1{1000}$, and so on. From 2.1 these are the negative powers $10^{-1},10^{-2},10^{-3},\ldots$.

A decimal is a sum of digits times powers of ten. Theorem 1 says that this makes every terminating decimal a fraction, with a denominator that is a power of ten.
Theorem 1: a terminating decimal is a fraction over a power of ten

If a decimal has $k$ digits after the point, then it equals $\dfrac N{10^k}$, where $N$ is the integer formed by all its digits with the point removed. Conversely, every fraction $\dfrac N{10^k}$ has a decimal with at most $k$ digits after the point. Multiplying a decimal by $10^k$ moves the point $k$ places to the right; dividing moves it $k$ places to the left.

What this theorem says, with examples

Example 1: sprint times

$11.43=\dfrac{1143}{100}$ and $11.4=\dfrac{114}{10}=\dfrac{1140}{100}$. The common denominator shows $1140<1143$.

Example 2: decimal to simplest fraction

$0.375=\dfrac{375}{1000}$. The HCF of $375$ and $1000$ is $125$, so $0.375=\dfrac38$.

Example 3: a fraction over a power of ten

$\dfrac{7}{1000}=0.007$: seven thousandths, so the $7$ sits in the third place.

Example 4: moving the point

$0.00472\times10^4=47.2$ and $360\div10^3=0.36$.

Show the proof (optional reading for the curious)
Place value. A decimal such as $d_1d_2\ldots d_k$ after the point means $\dfrac{d_1}{10}+\dfrac{d_2}{100}+\cdots+\dfrac{d_k}{10^k}$.
Common denominator. Over the common denominator $10^k$ this is $\dfrac{d_1\times10^{k-1}+d_2\times10^{k-2}+\cdots+d_k}{10^k}$. The top is exactly the integer $N$ written with digits $d_1d_2\ldots d_k$ (any whole-number part joins the front).
Shifting. Multiplying $\dfrac N{10^k}$ by $10^j$ gives $\dfrac N{10^{k-j}}$, which has $j$ fewer places: the point has moved $j$ places right. Dividing is the reverse. $\blacksquare$

Try it: the Place Value Machine

Technique 1: read, write and compare decimals
  1. Say the place of each digit: tenths, hundredths, thousandths, …
  2. To compare, pad with zeros so both have the same number of places, then compare as whole numbers. (Zeros at the end of a decimal do not change its value: $0.4=0.40=0.400$.)
  3. To convert to a fraction, write the digits over $10^k$ and simplify with the HCF.

Example 1: Order $0.409,\ 0.41,\ 0.4009,\ 0.4091$. Pad to four places: $0.4090,\ 0.4100,\ 0.4009,\ 0.4091$. Increasing: $0.4009<0.4090<0.4091<0.4100$.

Example 2: $2.64=\dfrac{264}{100}=\dfrac{66}{25}$ (divide top and bottom by $4$). As a mixed number it is $2\dfrac{16}{25}$.

Example 3: $0.0125=\dfrac{125}{10000}=\dfrac1{80}$ (the HCF is $125$).

2

Decimal arithmetic is fraction arithmetic

You already know how to add, multiply and divide fractions (2.3). A decimal is a fraction with denominator $10^k$, so the usual decimal rules are the fraction rules in disguise. Here are the three rules and why each one is true.

Theorem 2: the rules for decimal arithmetic

(i) Add or subtract: line up the decimal points. (ii) Multiply: multiply the numbers as integers; the answer has as many decimal places as the two factors together. (iii) Divide: multiply the dividend and the divisor by the same power of ten so the divisor is a whole number; the quotient does not change.

What this theorem says, with examples

Example 1: adding

$3.45+12.8-0.275$: write $3.450+12.800-0.275=16.025$.

Example 2: multiplying

$0.36\times0.25$: $36\times25=900$. There are $2+2=4$ decimal places, so the answer is $0.0900=0.09$.

Example 3: using a known product

Given $237\times46=10902$, then $2.37\times0.46=1.0902$ (two places plus two places make four places).

Example 4: dividing

$7.56\div0.12$: multiply both by $100$ to get $756\div12=63$.

Show the proof (optional reading for the curious)
Adding. $\dfrac a{10^k}+\dfrac b{10^k}=\dfrac{a+b}{10^k}$ (same denominator, Theorem 1 of 2.3). Lining up the points is the same as giving all the numbers the same $10^k$.
Multiplying. $\dfrac a{10^j}\times\dfrac b{10^k}=\dfrac{ab}{10^{j+k}}$ (Theorem 3 of 2.3 and the index law $10^j\times10^k=10^{j+k}$). So the integer $ab$ has $j+k$ places.
Dividing. $x\div y=\dfrac{x\times10^m}{y\times10^m}$ for any $m$, because multiplying top and bottom by the same non-zero number does not change a fraction (Theorem 1 of 2.2). Choose $m$ so that $y\times10^m$ is a whole number. $\blacksquare$
Technique 2: decimal word problems

Write down the operation, then do the calculation by the rule above, and finally check that the answer has a sensible size by rounding each number to one significant figure.

Example 1: Three notebooks cost $\$2.35$ each and two pens cost $\$1.80$ each. The change from $\$20$: $3\times2.35=7.05$ and $2\times1.80=3.60$, total $10.65$, so the change is $20-10.65=\$9.35$.

Example 2: A $1.8$ m ribbon is cut into pieces of $0.12$ m. $1.8\div0.12=180\div12=15$ pieces.

Step 2: From Fractions to Decimals: Stop or Repeat?

Some fractions turn into tidy decimals such as $\dfrac14=0.25$. Others never stop: $\dfrac13=0.3333\ldots$ and $\dfrac17=0.142857142857\ldots$ What decides which? The answer uses two ideas from Chapter 1: remainders (1.3) and prime factors (1.2).

1

Real life first: sharing one dollar

Scene 1: three, four and seven friends

You share $\$1$ fairly. With $4$ friends each gets $\$0.25$: the sharing finishes exactly. With $3$ friends each gets $\$0.33$ and a cent is left over. You cannot split that cent three ways, but you can split tenths of it: that gives another $3$ in the next place, and a left-over again, and so on forever. With $7$ friends the left-overs go round in a loop: $0.14\;28\;57\;14\;28\;57\ldots$

This is exactly long division. At each step we have a remainder. Multiply it by $10$, divide by the denominator, write the quotient digit, and keep the new remainder. If the remainder ever becomes $0$ the decimal stops. If a remainder ever repeats, then everything after it repeats too, because the next digit depends only on the current remainder.

Try it: the Long Division Stepper

/
Theorem 3: every rational number has a decimal that stops or repeats

For integers $a$ and $b>0$, the decimal expansion of $\dfrac ab$ either terminates or eventually repeats a block of digits. The repeating block has at most $b-1$ digits.

What this theorem says, with examples

Example 1: stops

$\dfrac78=0.875$. The remainders are $6,\ 4,\ 0$, and $0$ ends it.

Example 2: repeats at once

$\dfrac13=0.\overline3$. The only remainder is $1$, every time.

Example 3: repeats after a loop

$\dfrac17=0.\overline{142857}$. The remainders are $3,2,6,4,5,1,3,2,\ldots$: all six non-zero remainders appear before $1$ comes back.

Example 4: a late start

$\dfrac5{12}=0.41\overline6$. The remainders are $2,\ 8,\ 8,\ 8,\ldots$: once the remainder $8$ appears, it never changes, so the repeat starts at the third place.

Show the proof (optional reading for the curious)
Remainders are few. Each remainder is one of $0,1,2,\ldots,b-1$, so there are only $b$ possible values.
Zero or a repeat. If a remainder is $0$, the division ends. Otherwise, after at most $b$ steps two remainders must be equal (the pigeonhole principle: more steps than possible remainders).
Everything after repeats. The next digit and the next remainder are determined by the current remainder alone. So from the first repeated remainder onwards, the digits repeat in a loop. The loop uses only non-zero remainders, so it has at most $b-1$ digits. $\blacksquare$
Technique 3: convert a fraction to a decimal by long division
  1. Write the whole part, then keep the remainder.
  2. Multiply the remainder by $10$, divide by $b$: write the quotient digit and keep the new remainder.
  3. Stop at remainder $0$, or when a remainder repeats. Put a bar over the repeating block.

Example: $\dfrac3{16}$. $30\div16=1$ r $14$; $140\div16=8$ r $12$; $120\div16=7$ r $8$; $80\div16=5$ r $0$. So $\dfrac3{16}=0.1875$.

2

Which denominators stop? Prime factors decide

Scene 2: the pattern in a table

Try $\dfrac12,\dfrac14,\dfrac15,\dfrac18,\dfrac1{10},\dfrac1{16},\dfrac1{20},\dfrac1{25}$: all stop. Then $\dfrac13,\dfrac16,\dfrac17,\dfrac19,\dfrac1{11},\dfrac1{12},\dfrac1{14},\dfrac1{15}$: none stops. The stopping denominators are $2,4,5,8,10,16,20,25$, which are made only of the primes $2$ and $5$. The failing denominators all contain another prime: $3$, $7$, or $11$.

This is not a coincidence. A decimal that stops is a fraction over $10^k=2^k\times5^k$. So only the primes $2$ and $5$ can be hiding in the denominator. Theorem 4 makes this exact, and also counts the decimal places.
Theorem 4: when does a fraction terminate?

Let $\dfrac ab$ be in simplest form with $b>0$. Then $\dfrac ab$ is a terminating decimal if and only if $b=2^m\times5^n$ for some $m,n\ge0$. In that case the number of decimal places is exactly $\max(m,n)$.

What this theorem says, with examples

Example 1: count the places

$\dfrac7{8}$: $8=2^3$, so $\max(3,0)=3$ places: $0.875$. And $\dfrac{13}{40}$: $40=2^3\times5$, so $3$ places: $0.325$.

Example 2: the bigger exponent wins

$\dfrac3{64}$: $64=2^6$, so $6$ places: $0.046875$. And $\dfrac7{2^3\times5^5}$ has $\max(3,5)=5$ places.

Example 3: simplify first

$\dfrac{21}{70}$ contains the prime $7$ in $70=2\times5\times7$, but $\dfrac{21}{70}=\dfrac3{10}$ in simplest form, so it does terminate: $0.3$.

Example 4: a failure

$\dfrac5{12}$: $12=2^2\times3$ has the prime $3$, so it does not terminate.

Show the proof (optional reading for the curious)
If $b=2^m5^n$, it terminates. Let $k=\max(m,n)$. Multiply top and bottom by $2^{k-m}5^{k-n}$: the bottom becomes $2^k5^k=10^k$. By Theorem 1, $\dfrac ab=\dfrac N{10^k}$ has at most $k$ places.
If it terminates, $b=2^m5^n$. Suppose $\dfrac ab=\dfrac N{10^k}$. Then $a\times10^k=b\times N$, so $b$ divides $a\times10^k$. Since $\dfrac ab$ is in simplest form, $a$ and $b$ share no prime, so $b$ divides $10^k=2^k5^k$. By the Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic (1.2), the only primes that can divide $b$ are $2$ and $5$.
Exactly $\max(m,n)$ places. The fraction has at most $j$ places precisely when $b$ divides $10^j$, that is $j\ge m$ and $j\ge n$. The least such $j$ is $\max(m,n)$. $\blacksquare$
Technique 4: decide at once whether a fraction terminates
  1. Simplify the fraction first.
  2. Factorise the denominator. Only $2$s and $5$s means it terminates; the places are the larger of the two exponents.
  3. Anything else means it repeats (and Tab 3 and Tab 4 show how to find the repeating block).

Example 1: Which of $\dfrac7{12},\dfrac5{14},\dfrac9{40},\dfrac4{15}$ terminates? $12=2^2\times3$ (no), $14=2\times7$ (no), $40=2^3\times5$ (yes), $15=3\times5$ (no). Only $\dfrac9{40}=0.225$.

Example 2: How many of $\dfrac1n$ for $n=1,\ldots,20$ terminate? The $n$ made only of $2$s and $5$s are $1,2,4,5,8,10,16,20$: $\mathbf8$ of them.

3

A surprise: halves and the digits of $5^n$

Scene 3: the powers-of-5 connection

In 2.1 you counted the digits of $2^{18}\times5^{12}$ and $5^{130}$. Decimals give a second view of the same idea. Since $\dfrac1{2^n}=\dfrac{5^n}{10^n}$, the decimal for $\dfrac1{2^n}$ is the number $5^n$ shifted $n$ places to the right, so it has exactly $n$ decimal places and always ends in $5$.

Example. $\dfrac1{2^6}=\dfrac1{64}=\dfrac{15625}{10^6}=0.015625$: $6$ places, and the digits are those of $5^6=15625$ with a leading zero. Similarly $\dfrac1{2^{10}}=0.0009765625$ has $10$ places; its leading zeros are the places that $5^{10}=9765625$ does not need, so $5^{10}$ has $10-3=7$ digits. And $2^{18}\times5^{12}=2^6\times10^{12}=64\times10^{12}$, a number with $14$ digits.

Step 3: Recurring Decimals Are Fractions Too

We now go the other way: given a recurring decimal such as $0.\overline{36}$, which fraction is it? You will meet a clever multiply and subtract trick, and settle a famous argument: is $0.999\ldots$ equal to $1$?

1

Real life first: a bar that never ends

Scene 1: the stopwatch that cannot stop

A shared timer shows $0.3333\ldots$ of a minute. Is that a real, exact amount of time? Yes: it is $\dfrac13$ of a minute, which is $20$ seconds. The infinite string of $3$s is just the way a decimal tries to write the fraction $\dfrac13$.

Notation: a bar over the digits that repeat. $0.\overline3=0.333\ldots$, $0.\overline{36}=0.363636\ldots$ and $0.1\overline6=0.16666\ldots$ The digits under the bar are the repeating block; the digits before the bar are the non-repeating part.

The trick for turning a bar into a fraction is to shift the decimal so the repeating tails line up, then subtract so the tails vanish. Theorem 5 states the result for a pure repeating block.
Theorem 5: a repeating block is a fraction over $10^k-1$

If a block of $k$ digits forms the integer $N$, then $$0.\overline{N}=\frac N{10^k-1}=\frac N{99\ldots9}\quad(k\text{ nines}).$$ A block that follows a non-repeating part of $j$ digits is obtained by shifting: first find the fraction for the block, then divide by $10^j$ and add the non-repeating part.

What this theorem says, with examples

Example 1: one digit

$0.\overline7=\dfrac79$. And $0.\overline3=\dfrac39=\dfrac13$.

Example 2: two digits

$0.\overline{36}=\dfrac{36}{99}=\dfrac4{11}$ (divide by $9$).

Example 3: three digits, with leading zeros

$0.\overline{027}=\dfrac{27}{999}=\dfrac1{37}$ (divide by $27$). The leading zero is part of the block.

Example 4: a whole part

$2.\overline{45}=2+\dfrac{45}{99}=2+\dfrac5{11}=\dfrac{27}{11}$.

Show the proof (optional reading for the curious)
Name it and shift. Let $x=0.\overline N$. Shifting the point $k$ places gives $10^kx=N.\overline N$, which is $N+x$ (the same tail comes after the whole number $N$).
Subtract. $10^kx-x=N$, so $(10^k-1)x=N$ and $x=\dfrac N{10^k-1}$. $\blacksquare$
Why subtracting is legal. Both $10^kx$ and $x$ have the identical infinite tail, so term by term it cancels exactly.

Try it: the Recurring Decimal Converter

In the buttons, brackets mark the repeating block: $0.1(6)$ means $0.1\overline6$.

Technique 5: recurring decimal to fraction (the multiply and subtract method)
  1. Call the number $x$.
  2. Multiply by $10^j$ to put the non-repeating digits to the left of the point ($j$ = number of non-repeating digits).
  3. Multiply by $10^{j+k}$ to move one full block more ($k$ = length of the block). The two tails now match.
  4. Subtract the two equations, and solve for $x$. Simplify.

Example 1: $x=0.1\overline6$. $10x=1.\overline6$ and $100x=16.\overline6$. Subtract: $90x=15$, so $x=\dfrac{15}{90}=\dfrac16$.

Example 2: $x=0.2\overline3$. $10x=2.\overline3$, $100x=23.\overline3$. Subtract: $90x=21$, so $x=\dfrac{21}{90}=\dfrac7{30}$.

Example 3: $x=0.58\overline3$. $100x=58.\overline3$, $1000x=583.\overline3$. Subtract: $900x=525$, so $x=\dfrac{525}{900}=\dfrac7{12}$.

Example 4 (arithmetic with recurring decimals): $0.\overline{12}+0.\overline{21}=\dfrac{12}{99}+\dfrac{21}{99}=\dfrac{33}{99}=\dfrac13$. Convert to fractions first; the arithmetic of 2.3 then does the work.

The famous question: is $0.999\ldots=1$?

Scene 2: the argument in the classroom

Many students are sure that $0.999\ldots$ is “just below” $1$, getting closer and closer but never arriving. Others say it equals $1$. Who is right? Theorem 5 settles it: $0.\overline9=\dfrac99=1$.

Theorem 6: $0.\overline9=1$

The recurring decimal $0.999\ldots$ is another name for the number $1$. Likewise every terminating decimal has a second form ending in $9$s: $0.5=0.4\overline9$ and $2.37=2.36\overline9$.

What this theorem says, with examples

Example 1: from thirds

$\dfrac13=0.\overline3$, so $3\times\dfrac13=3\times0.\overline3=0.\overline9$. But $3\times\dfrac13=1$.

Example 2: a second name for $0.5$

$0.4\overline9=0.4+\dfrac1{10}\times0.\overline9=0.4+0.1=0.5$.

Example 3: no room between

In 2.2 we proved there is always a fraction between two different numbers. Try to find a number strictly between $0.\overline9$ and $1$: there is none (the digits $9,9,9,\ldots$ never let the number fall below $1$ by any amount). So they are not two different numbers.

Show the proof (optional reading for the curious)
Multiply and subtract. Let $x=0.\overline9$. Then $10x=9.\overline9$. Subtract: $10x-x=9$, so $9x=9$ and $x=1$.
The gap argument. Suppose $x=0.\overline9<1$. Then $1-x>0$, but $1-x$ is smaller than $\dfrac1{10^n}$ for every $n$ (the first $n$ digits of $x$ are $9$s), and the only non-negative number smaller than every $\dfrac1{10^n}$ is $0$. So $1-x=0$. $\blacksquare$

Step 4: The Cycle of $\tfrac17$ and the Clock of Remainders

Why does $\dfrac17$ repeat after exactly six digits, $\dfrac1{11}$ after two, and $\dfrac1{13}$ after six? The answer is the modular arithmetic of 1.3: the repeating block is a clock of remainders, and its length is how long $10^k$ takes to return to $1$ on a clock with $b$ hours.

1

Real life first: the week and the wheel

Scene 1: seven days, six digits

A week has $7$ days. If today is Monday, which day is it in $10$ days? In $100$ days? In $1000$ days? On the clock of $7$ the powers of ten land at $10\equiv3$, $100\equiv2$, $1000\equiv6$, $10^4\equiv4$, $10^5\equiv5$, $10^6\equiv1$ (mod $7$). Six steps and you are back at $1$, then everything repeats.

These are exactly the remainders you saw in the Long Division Stepper for $\dfrac17$: $3,2,6,4,5,1$. The digit written at each step is the quotient: $1,4,2,8,5,7$. After six steps the remainder is $1$ again, so the block $142857$ starts over.

The number of digits in the repeating block of $\dfrac1b$ is the number of steps $10^k$ needs to come back to $1$ on the clock of $b$. Theorem 7 turns this into a rule.
Theorem 7: the period of $\dfrac ab$

Let $\dfrac ab$ be in simplest form, and write $b=2^m5^n\,c$ with $c$ coprime to $10$. If $c=1$ the decimal terminates. Otherwise the decimal has exactly $s=\max(m,n)$ digits before the repeating block starts, and the repeating block has length $k$, where $k$ is the smallest positive integer with $10^k\equiv1\pmod c$, that is, with $c\mid10^k-1$.

What this theorem says, with examples

Example 1: $\dfrac17$

$c=7$. Powers of ten mod $7$: $3,2,6,4,5,1$. The first return to $1$ is at $k=6$. So the block has $6$ digits.

Example 2: small periods

$\dfrac13$: $10\equiv1$, so $k=1$. $\dfrac1{11}$: $10\equiv-1$ and $100\equiv1$, so $k=2$. $\dfrac1{37}$: $999=27\times37$, so $k=3$. $\dfrac1{101}$: $9999=99\times101$, so $k=4$.

Example 3: a mixed denominator

$\dfrac1{12}$: $12=2^2\times3$, so $s=2$ and $c=3$ with $k=1$. $\dfrac1{12}=0.08\overline3$: two digits, then $3$ repeating. And $\dfrac1{21}$: $c=21$, powers of $10$ mod $21$ are $10,16,13,4,19,1$, so $k=6$: $\dfrac1{21}=0.\overline{047619}$.

Example 4: the pre-period is made by the $2$s and $5$s

$\dfrac1{6}=0.1\overline6$ has $s=1$, and $\dfrac7{30}=0.2\overline3$ has $s=1$ ($30=2\times3\times5$).

Show the proof (optional reading for the curious)
A pure repeat when $\gcd(b,10)=1$. Start the long division of $\dfrac ab$ with remainder $a$. After $k$ digits the remainder is $a\times10^k\bmod b$. The digits repeat from the start exactly when this remainder equals $a$ again, that is $a(10^k-1)\equiv0\pmod b$, which (as $\gcd(a,b)=1$) means $b\mid10^k-1$. Such a $k$ exists: the powers $10,10^2,\ldots$ cannot all be different mod $b$, and $10^i\equiv10^j$ with $\gcd(10,b)=1$ lets us cancel to get $10^{j-i}\equiv1$.
Removing the $2$s and $5$s. Let $s=\max(m,n)$. Then $10^s\times\dfrac ab=\dfrac Ac$ where $A$ is an integer coprime to $c$ (we multiplied by $2^s5^s$, which cancels $2^m5^n$ and leaves only $2$s and $5$s on top). Write $\dfrac Ac=w+\dfrac rc$, with $0
Why the pre-period is exactly $s$. A purely repeating decimal equals $\dfrac N{10^k-1}$ (Theorem 5), whose denominator in simplest form is coprime to $10$. If $\dfrac ab$ started to repeat earlier than $s$ places, shifting by one fewer place, $10^{s-1}\dfrac ab$ would be a purely repeating number plus an integer with a denominator still divisible by $2$ or $5$. That is impossible. $\blacksquare$

Try it: the Period Finder

Technique 7: find the period using powers of ten
  1. Simplify the fraction, and remove the $2$s and $5$s from the denominator to leave $c$.
  2. Compute $10,\ 10^2,\ 10^3,\ \ldots$ modulo $c$ (each time multiply the last remainder by $10$ and reduce).
  3. The first time you reach $1$ is the period $k$.

Example: $\dfrac1{13}$. $10$, then $100\equiv9$, $90\equiv12$, $120\equiv3$, $30\equiv4$, $40\equiv1$. That is $k=6$: $\dfrac1{13}=0.\overline{076923}$.

Technique 8: find a far-away digit, or a sum of digits
  1. Write the non-repeating part and the block.
  2. Subtract the pre-period length from the position, then divide by the period. The remainder says which digit of the block it is (remainder $0$ means the last digit of the block).
  3. For sums: count full blocks, then add the leftover digits.

Example 1: The $2026$th digit of $\dfrac17=0.\overline{142857}$. $2026=6\times337+4$, so it is the $4$th digit of the block: $\mathbf8$.

Example 2: The sum of the first $100$ digits of $\dfrac17$. $100=6\times16+4$. Each block sums to $1+4+2+8+5+7=27$, so $16\times27=432$, plus the next four digits $1+4+2+8=15$. Total $\mathbf{447}$.

Example 3 (with a pre-period): The $50$th digit of $\dfrac5{12}=0.41\overline6$. The first $2$ digits are $4,1$; every digit after that is $6$. So the $50$th digit is $\mathbf6$.

The cyclic number

Scene 2: a number that rotates

The block $142857$ has a strange property. Multiply it by $2,3,4,5,6$: $142857\times2=285714$, $\times3=428571$, $\times4=571428$, $\times5=714285$, $\times6=857142$. The same six digits appear in a rotated order! And $\times7=999999$.

Why? The decimal of $\dfrac27$ is $2\times0.\overline{142857}=0.\overline{285714}$. The fractions $\dfrac17,\dfrac27,\ldots,\dfrac67$ use the six remainders $1,\ldots,6$ in one big loop, so each of their decimals is the loop started in a different place.

Step 5: Rounding, Bounds and Smart Thinking

Most decimals in real life are approximations. This tab teaches how to round correctly, how to say exactly how wrong a rounded value can be, and how to use decimals cleverly in competition questions. It ends with an optional Investigation Lab where you discover Theorem 3 for yourself.

1

Rounding and significant figures

Scene 1: the photo finish

A stopwatch shows $11.4386$ s. The official result is only recorded to $2$ decimal places: $11.44$ s. A news report says “about $11$ seconds”. All three numbers describe the same race, but with different precision. Rounding is the honest way to give up some digits.

Theorem 8: rounding

To $n$ decimal places: look at the digit in the $(n+1)$th place. If it is $5$ or more, add $1$ to the $n$th digit (carrying if needed); otherwise leave the $n$th digit alone. Then drop everything after the $n$th place.

To $s$ significant figures: the first significant figure is the first non-zero digit from the left. Count $s$ digits from there and round at that place. Zeros before the first non-zero digit are never significant.

Truncating to $n$ places simply drops the later digits without adding $1$ ever.

What this theorem says, with examples

Example 1: decimal places

$3.14159$ to $2$ d.p.: the third digit is $1$, so $\mathbf{3.14}$. To $4$ d.p.: the fifth digit is $9$, so round up: $\mathbf{3.1416}$.

Example 2: carrying

$2.9996$ to $2$ d.p.: the third digit is $9$, so add $1$ to $2.99$ and get $\mathbf{3.00}$. Keep the zeros: they show the precision.

Example 3: significant figures

$0.004083$ to $2$ s.f.: the first significant figure is $4$, the second is $0$, the next digit is $8$ so round up: $\mathbf{0.0041}$. And $5\,382$ to $2$ s.f. is $\mathbf{5400}$ (the zeros are place-holders).

Example 4: truncating is different

$7.3968$ truncated to $2$ d.p. is $7.39$, but rounded to $2$ d.p. is $7.40$.

Try it: the Rounding Machine

Technique 9: round correctly
  1. Mark the place where you must stop (underline that digit).
  2. Look only at the next one digit. Do not round twice: $2.3452$ to $1$ d.p. is $2.3$, not $2.4$.
  3. Write the zeros that show the precision, and state the unit of precision.

Bounds: how wrong can a rounded number be?

Theorem 9: bounds of a rounded value

If $x$ is rounded to a place whose unit is $u$ (for example $u=0.01$ for $2$ d.p.), the true value lies in $\left[\,x-\tfrac u2,\ x+\tfrac u2\right)$. That is, the error is at most half a unit. The lower bound is included and the upper bound is not.

What this theorem says, with examples

Example 1

A length is $4.7$ cm, to $1$ d.p. Then $4.65\le L<4.75$.

Example 2: using bounds

A rectangle is $4.7$ cm by $3.2$ cm, both to $1$ d.p. The largest possible area is just under $4.75\times3.25=15.4375$, and the smallest is $4.65\times3.15=14.6475$.

Technique 10: make the bounds work for you
  1. Write each rounded number as an interval with half-unit error.
  2. For a sum or product of positives, use all lower bounds for the smallest result and all upper bounds for the largest.
  3. For a difference or a quotient, mix them: the largest $a-b$ uses the largest $a$ and the smallest $b$.

Example: $a=8.6$ and $b=2.4$ to $1$ d.p. The largest $a-b$ is $8.65-2.35=\mathbf{6.30}$ (just under), and the smallest is $8.55-2.45=\mathbf{6.10}$.

Smart thinking with decimals

Scene 2: three tricks that save a calculator

Trick 1: multiply by a power of ten to clear the point. $0.36\times0.25=\dfrac{36\times25}{10000}=\dfrac{900}{10000}=0.09$. And $1.2\div0.04=\dfrac{120}{4}=30$.

Trick 2: use fractions you know. $0.125=\dfrac18$, $0.375=\dfrac38$, $0.2\overline3=\dfrac7{30}$. So $48\times0.375=48\times\dfrac38=18$.

Trick 3: look for sums of $1$. $0.\overline3+0.\overline6=1$ and $0.\overline{27}+0.\overline{72}=1$, because $\dfrac3{11}+\dfrac8{11}=1$.

Calculators round! If a display shows $0.3333333333$ it is not $\dfrac13$, and $0.9999999999$ is not $1$. Use the calculator for checking, and algebra for deciding.
