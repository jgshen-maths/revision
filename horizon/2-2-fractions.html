Step 1: Sharing, and the Birth of Fractions

In 2.1 you met negative and zero indices, and you already saw $2^{-3}=\tfrac18$ appear. Now we meet the numbers properly. Where do fractions come from, when are two fractions the same number, and what is the “simplest” way to write one?

1

Real life first: sharing three pizzas among four people

Scene 1: the pizza problem

Three equal pizzas must be shared fairly among four friends. Each friend should get the same amount. Everyone wants to know: how much is that?

  • Plan A. Cut every pizza into $4$ equal slices. That makes $12$ slices, and each friend takes $3$ slices, one from each pizza.
  • Plan B. Cut every pizza into $8$ equal slices: $24$ slices, so each friend takes $6$ slices.
  • Plan C. Cut every pizza into $12$ equal slices: $36$ slices, so each friend takes $9$ slices.

Plan A gives each friend three quarters of a pizza, Plan B six eighths and Plan C nine twelfths. The slices look different, but a friend gets exactly the same amount of pizza every time.

So three different-looking fractions, $\tfrac34$, $\tfrac68$ and $\tfrac9{12}$, name the same number. Two questions follow. (1) When exactly are two fractions the same number? (2) Is there one best way to write it?

Why we needed a new kind of number. In 1.1 you learned that $\mathbb{Z}$ is closed under $+$, $-$ and $\times$. It is not closed under division: $3\div4$ has no answer in $\mathbb{Z}$, because no integer $x$ makes $4\times x=3$. The pizza problem is exactly this division.

Definition. For integers $a$ and $b$ with $b\ne0$, the fraction $\dfrac ab$ is the number $x$ that answers the question “$b\times x=a$”, that is, the answer to $a\div b$. The set of all such numbers is the set of rational numbers:

$$\mathbb{Q}=\left\{\frac ab:\ a\in\mathbb{Z},\ b\in\mathbb{Z},\ b\ne0\right\}.$$

  • Every integer is a fraction: $n=\dfrac n1$. So $\mathbb{N}\subset\mathbb{Z}\subset\mathbb{Q}$, a chain of sets, each inside the next.
  • $a$ is the numerator (how many pieces) and $b$ is the denominator (the name of the piece: how many equal parts make one whole).
  • Why $b\ne0$? We would need a number $x$ with $0\times x=a$. If $a\ne0$ no such number exists, and if $a=0$ every number works. Either way $\tfrac a0$ means nothing.
Theorem 1: when are two fractions equal?

(i) Multiplying or dividing the numerator and denominator by the same non-zero number does not change the fraction: $\dfrac ab=\dfrac{ka}{kb}$.

(ii) Cross-multiplication test: $\dfrac ab=\dfrac cd$ exactly when $ad=bc$.

What this theorem says, with examples

Example 1: the pizza fractions

$\dfrac34=\dfrac{3\times2}{4\times2}=\dfrac68=\dfrac{3\times3}{4\times3}=\dfrac9{12}$. Check with the test: $3\times8=24=4\times6$ ✓.

Example 2: a quick test

Are $\dfrac{4}{6}$ and $\dfrac{10}{15}$ equal? Cross-multiply: $4\times15=60$ and $6\times10=60$. Yes, both equal $\tfrac23$.

Example 3: a test that fails

Are $\dfrac5{7}$ and $\dfrac{15}{20}$ equal? $5\times20=100$ but $7\times15=105$. No. (They are close: $0.714\ldots$ and $0.75$.)

Example 4: finding a missing number

$\dfrac37=\dfrac{?}{35}$. The denominator was multiplied by $5$ ($7\to35$), so the numerator must be too: $3\times5=15$.

Show the proof (optional reading for the curious)
Part (i). $\dfrac ab$ is the number $x$ with $bx=a$. Multiply both sides by $k$: $(kb)x=ka$. So the same $x$ answers $\dfrac{ka}{kb}$.
Part (ii), forward. If $\dfrac ab=\dfrac cd$, multiply both sides by $bd$ (not zero): $ad=cb$.
Part (ii), backward. If $ad=bc$, divide both sides by $bd$: $\dfrac ab=\dfrac cd$. $\blacksquare$
Meaning. A rational number is a whole family of equal-looking fractions, like the residue classes of 1.3: $\left\{\dfrac34,\dfrac68,\dfrac9{12},\dfrac{-3}{-4},\ldots\right\}$.

The simplest form is unique

Theorem 2: every fraction has exactly one simplest form

Every rational number can be written as $\dfrac ab$ with $b>0$ and $\text{HCF}(a,b)=1$, and there is only one way to do so. To find it, divide the numerator and denominator by their HCF.

What this theorem says, with examples

Example 1: divide by the HCF

$\dfrac{84}{126}$: $84=2^2\times3\times7$ and $126=2\times3^2\times7$, so $\text{HCF}=2\times3\times7=42$. Then $\dfrac{84}{126}=\dfrac{84\div42}{126\div42}=\dfrac23$.

Example 2: negative fractions

$\dfrac{-6}{9}=\dfrac{-2}3$ and also $\dfrac{6}{-9}=\dfrac{-2}{3}$. By convention we keep the denominator positive and put the minus sign in the numerator (or in front): $-\dfrac23$.

Example 3: how many fractions $\dfrac a{12}$ are already in simplest form?

For $a=1,2,\ldots,11$, the fraction is simplest exactly when $\text{HCF}(a,12)=1$. Since $12=2^2\times3$, $a$ must not be divisible by $2$ or $3$: $a=1,5,7,11$. That is $4$ fractions: $\tfrac1{12},\tfrac5{12},\tfrac7{12},\tfrac{11}{12}$.

Show the proof (optional reading for the curious)
Existence. Let $g=\text{HCF}(a,b)$. Then $a=ga'$ and $b=gb'$ with $\text{HCF}(a',b')=1$, and $\dfrac ab=\dfrac{a'}{b'}$ by Theorem 1. Multiply top and bottom by $-1$ if needed to make the denominator positive.
Uniqueness. Suppose $\dfrac ab=\dfrac cd$ with both fractions in simplest form and $b,d>0$. By Theorem 1, $ad=bc$. Then $b$ divides $ad$. Since $\text{HCF}(a,b)=1$, $b$ has no prime factor in common with $a$, so by the Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic (1.2) all the prime factors of $b$ must lie in $d$: $b\mid d$. In the same way $d\mid b$. Two positive integers that divide each other are equal, so $b=d$, and then $a=c$. $\blacksquare$
Technique 1: simplify a fraction

Find the HCF of the numerator and denominator (prime factors, as in 1.2), then divide both by it. If you do not see the HCF at once, cancel any common factor you can see and repeat until the HCF of the new numbers is $1$.

Example 1: $\dfrac{144}{360}$. $144=2^4\times3^2$ and $360=2^3\times3^2\times5$, so the HCF is $2^3\times3^2=72$ and the answer is $\dfrac{2}{5}$.

Example 2 (cancel in steps): $\dfrac{90}{150}\to\dfrac{9}{15}$ (divide by $10$) $\to\dfrac35$ (divide by $3$).

Technique 2: find a missing number, or work backwards from a ratio

If a fraction equals $\dfrac pq$ in simplest form, then the numerator and denominator are $kp$ and $kq$ for some whole number $k$. Use the information given to find $k$.

Example 1: A fraction equals $\dfrac38$ and its numerator and denominator add to $88$. Write it as $\dfrac{3k}{8k}$: $3k+8k=11k=88$, so $k=8$, and the fraction is $\dfrac{24}{64}$.

Example 2: A fraction equals $\dfrac27$ and the denominator is $15$ more than the numerator. $7k-2k=5k=15$, so $k=3$ and the fraction is $\dfrac{6}{21}$.

Step 2: Fractions on the Number Line, and Fractions as Operators

A fraction is a point on the number line as well as a piece of a pizza. Here you learn to place fractions, read scales, switch between improper fractions and mixed numbers, and use a fraction as an operator that acts on a quantity.

1

Placing a fraction on the number line

Rule. To place $\dfrac pq$ with $q>0$: cut each unit of the number line into $q$ equal gaps, then count $p$ gaps from $0$ (to the right if $p>0$, to the left if $p<0$).

  • Equal fractions land on the same point: $\dfrac34$ and $\dfrac68$ are one point, reached with a different number of smaller steps (Theorem 1).
  • An improper fraction has $p\ge q$ and lies at $1$ or further right. Write it as a mixed number with the division algorithm from 1.3: $\dfrac{17}5$: $17=5\times3+2$, so $\dfrac{17}5=3\dfrac25$, between $3$ and $4$.
  • For a negative fraction, divide the positive version, then reflect: $-\dfrac{17}5=-3\dfrac25=-3.4$, between $-4$ and $-3$. (Careful: it is not between $-3$ and $-2$.)

Try it: the Number Line Placer

Technique 3: read a scale (what is one small gap worth?)

Two marked values and some unmarked gaps in between: (difference between the marks) $\div$ (number of equal gaps) = the value of one gap. Then count gaps from a marked value. Count gaps, not ticks: there is one more tick than gaps.

Example 1: The marks $0$ and $2$ have $8$ equal gaps between them. One gap is $\dfrac{2-0}{8}=\dfrac14$. The point $5$ gaps from $0$ is $\dfrac54$.

Example 2 (marks not at zero): Marks $\dfrac13$ and $\dfrac23$ with $5$ equal gaps. The difference is $\dfrac23-\dfrac13=\dfrac13$, and one gap is $\dfrac13\div5=\dfrac1{15}$. The point $2$ gaps above $\dfrac13$ is $\dfrac13+\dfrac2{15}=\dfrac5{15}+\dfrac2{15}=\dfrac7{15}$.

Example 3 (placing a given fraction): Marks $\dfrac12$ and $\dfrac34$ with $5$ equal gaps. One gap is $\dfrac14\div5=\dfrac1{20}$. The fraction $\dfrac35=0.6$ is $\dfrac35-\dfrac12=\dfrac1{10}=\dfrac2{20}$ above $\dfrac12$, so at gap number $2$.

Technique 4: change between improper fractions and mixed numbers

Improper to mixed: divide the numerator by the denominator: the quotient is the whole part, the remainder is the new numerator. Mixed to improper: whole $\times$ denominator + numerator, over the denominator.

Example 1: $\dfrac{47}6$: $47=6\times7+5$, so $7\dfrac56$.

Example 2: $3\dfrac27=\dfrac{3\times7+2}7=\dfrac{23}7$.

2

A fraction as an operator on a quantity

In 1.2 you learned that $3\times2$ means “apply the operator $3$ to the number $2$”: it stretches $2$ to three times its length. A fraction can be an operator too.

$\dfrac pq$ of a quantity $N$ means: divide $N$ into $q$ equal parts, then take $p$ of them. In symbols, $\dfrac pq\times N$. The operator $\dfrac pq$ does two small jobs: “$\div q$” shrinks, “$\times p$” stretches. The order does not matter.

Example: $\dfrac34$ of $20$. Shrink: $20\div4=5$ (one quarter). Stretch: $5\times3=15$. So $\dfrac34$ of $20=15$. On the number line from $0$ to $20$, you land $\dfrac34$ of the way along.

Technique 5: find a fraction of a quantity, and the fraction that a part is of a whole

Example 1 (fraction of): A tank holds $240$ litres. $\dfrac38$ is used. Used: $240\div8=30$ per eighth, $30\times3=90$ litres. Left: $240-90=150$ litres (or $\dfrac58$ of $240=150$).

Example 2 (what fraction): $18$ of the $30$ students walk to school. The fraction is $\dfrac{18}{30}$, which simplifies to $\dfrac35$ by Technique 1.

Technique 6: undo the operator (find the whole from a part)

If $\dfrac pq$ of a number is known, reverse the two jobs: undo the stretch by dividing by $p$ (that gives one $\dfrac1q$ part), then undo the shrink by multiplying by $q$.

Example 1: $\dfrac23$ of a number is $18$. Divide by $2$: $9$ is one third. Multiply by $3$: the number is $27$.

Example 2: $\dfrac35$ of a ribbon is $21$ cm. One fifth is $21\div3=7$ cm, so the whole ribbon is $35$ cm, and $\dfrac25$ of it is $14$ cm.

Step 3: Comparing and Ordering Fractions

Which fraction is bigger? You will meet four reliable tools: the cross-multiplication test, the common denominator, benchmarks, and a clever “gap” trick for fractions that are very close to $1$.

1

Real life first: who is the better shooter?

Scene 1: the basketball argument

In the school final, Ana made $7$ of her $9$ shots. Ben made $9$ of his $12$ shots. Both claim to be the better shooter. Ben made more baskets and took more shots, but that does not settle it. We need to compare the fractions $\dfrac79$ and $\dfrac9{12}$.

Different denominators mean different-sized pieces, so we cannot compare the numerators directly. Cut both into the same-size pieces. $9$ and $12$ both divide $36$: $\dfrac79=\dfrac{28}{36}$ and $\dfrac9{12}=\dfrac{27}{36}$. Now we count pieces of the same size: $28\gt27$, so Ana is the better shooter, by one thirty-sixth of a basket.

The idea is always the same: put the fractions on a common denominator, then compare numerators. Is there a faster way that does not need us to find the common denominator? Yes: Theorem 3.
Theorem 3: the cross-multiplication test

For positive denominators $b>0$ and $d>0$: $$\frac ab\lt\frac cd\iff ad\lt bc,\qquad \frac ab=\frac cd\iff ad=bc,\qquad \frac ab>\frac cd\iff ad>bc.$$ (The numbers $a$ and $c$ may be negative.)

What this theorem says, with examples

Example 1: Ana against Ben

$\dfrac79$ against $\dfrac9{12}$: $7\times12=84$ and $9\times9=81$. Since $84>81$, $\dfrac79>\dfrac9{12}$. ✓ (The cross products are $\tfrac{28}{36}$ and $\tfrac{27}{36}$ with the common $36$ removed.)

Example 2: close fractions

$\dfrac58$ against $\dfrac7{11}$: $5\times11=55$ and $8\times7=56$. So $\dfrac58\lt\dfrac7{11}$. (Decimals: $0.625$ and $0.636\ldots$)

Example 3: negative numerators

$-\dfrac35$ against $-\dfrac47$: $-3\times7=-21$ and $5\times(-4)=-20$. Since $-21\lt-20$, $-\dfrac35\lt-\dfrac47$. (The one with the bigger positive part is further left.)

Show the proof (optional reading for the curious)
Multiply by a positive number. Multiplying both sides of an inequality by a positive number keeps its direction. Since $bd>0$, multiply $\dfrac ab\lt\dfrac cd$ by $bd$: $a\,d\lt c\,b$.
Reverse. Dividing both sides of $ad\lt bc$ by the positive $bd$ gives back $\dfrac ab\lt\dfrac cd$. The cases $=$ and $>$ are identical. $\blacksquare$
Why $b,d>0$ matters. If a denominator were negative, multiplying by $bd$ would flip the inequality. So always put the minus sign in the numerator first.

Technique 7: compare or order with a common denominator

Use the LCM of the denominators (1.2). Rewrite every fraction with that denominator, then put the numerators in order. This is best for ordering three or more fractions.

Example: Order $\dfrac34,\ \dfrac57,\ \dfrac7{10},\ \dfrac23$. LCM of $4,7,10,3$ is $420$. $\dfrac34=\dfrac{315}{420}$, $\dfrac57=\dfrac{300}{420}$, $\dfrac7{10}=\dfrac{294}{420}$, $\dfrac23=\dfrac{280}{420}$. Increasing order: $\dfrac23\lt\dfrac7{10}\lt\dfrac57\lt\dfrac34$.

Technique 8: benchmarks, same numerator, same denominator
  • Same denominator: the larger numerator is larger. Same numerator (positive): the smaller denominator is larger, since fewer, bigger pieces: $\dfrac7{15}>\dfrac7{16}$.
  • Benchmarks. Compare each fraction with $0$, $\tfrac12$ or $1$. For example $\dfrac{9}{17}>\tfrac12$ (since $2\times9=18>17$) but $\dfrac{8}{17}\lt\tfrac12$, so $\dfrac9{17}>\dfrac8{17}$ with no calculation.

Example: Which is larger, $\dfrac{13}{25}$ or $\dfrac{14}{29}$? $\dfrac{13}{25}>\dfrac12$ because $26>25$, but $\dfrac{14}{29}\lt\dfrac12$ because $28\lt29$. So $\dfrac{13}{25}$ is larger.

2

Fractions just below $1$: compare the missing piece

Fractions such as $\dfrac{99}{100}$ or $\dfrac{998}{1001}$ are almost $1$. The cross-multiplication test works, but the numbers are huge. Instead write each as 1 minus the missing piece:

$$\frac{b-k}{b}=1-\frac kb.$$

The smaller the missing piece, the larger the fraction. So compare the gaps $\dfrac kb$ instead, and remember the order flips.

Technique 9: the gap-to-1 method
  1. Write each fraction as $1-\text{gap}$, where gap $=\dfrac{\text{denominator}-\text{numerator}}{\text{denominator}}$.
  2. Make each gap a unit fraction $\dfrac1{(\ldots)}$ by dividing top and bottom by the numerator of the gap. The gap with the bigger denominator is the smaller gap.
  3. Smaller gap means bigger fraction.

Example 1: Order $\dfrac45,\dfrac56,\dfrac67$. The gaps are $\dfrac15,\dfrac16,\dfrac17$. Gaps shrink in this order, so the fractions grow: $\dfrac45\lt\dfrac56\lt\dfrac67$.

Example 2: Which is larger, $\dfrac{7}{8}$ or $\dfrac{77}{87}$? Gaps: $\dfrac18=0.125$ and $\dfrac{10}{87}=\dfrac1{8.7}\approx0.115$. The second gap is smaller, so $\dfrac{77}{87}$ is larger.

Example 3 (gaps that are not unit fractions): Order $\dfrac{99}{100},\ \dfrac{999}{1001},\ \dfrac{9999}{10003}$. Gaps: $\dfrac1{100}$, $\dfrac{2}{1001}=\dfrac1{500.5}$, $\dfrac4{10003}=\dfrac1{2500.75}$. The gaps shrink, so the fractions increase in the order listed: $\dfrac{99}{100}\lt\dfrac{999}{1001}\lt\dfrac{9999}{10003}$.

Step 4: Between Any Two Fractions

After the integer $5$ comes $6$. But what comes after the fraction $\tfrac12$? In this tab you discover a surprising property of $\mathbb{Q}$: between any two fractions, however close, there is always another one, and then another, forever.

1

Real life first: zooming in on the finish line

Scene 1: two runners and a stopwatch

Two runners finish a race: Mia in $10.2$ seconds and Leo in $10.3$ seconds. The judges say Mia was first. Could a better stopwatch show a runner finishing between the two? Yes: $10.25$ seconds. And between $10.2$ and $10.25$? Yes: $10.225$. Every time we improve the watch, we can place a finishing time in the gap.

Now ask the same about fractions. What is the number just after $\dfrac12$? If it were $\dfrac{51}{100}$, then $\dfrac{501}{1000}$ is nearer. If it were $\dfrac{501}{1000}$, then $\dfrac{5001}{10000}$ is nearer. There is no next fraction, whatever you try.

In $\mathbb{Z}$ every number has a next number. In $\mathbb{Q}$ no number does. The numbers are “packed” so densely that the gaps never close. Mathematicians call this property density.
Theorem 4: the midpoint, and density of $\mathbb{Q}$

If $p\lt q$ are fractions, then their average $m=\dfrac{p+q}2$ is a fraction with $p\lt m\lt q$. Therefore there are infinitely many fractions between any two fractions.

What this theorem says, with examples

Example 1: halfway between two fractions

Halfway between $\dfrac14$ and $\dfrac56$. Common denominator $12$: $\dfrac3{12}$ and $\dfrac{10}{12}$. Add: $\dfrac{13}{12}$. Halve: $\dfrac{13}{24}$. Check: $\dfrac14=0.25$, $\dfrac56=0.8\overline3$, and $\dfrac{13}{24}=0.541\overline6$, which is the middle.

Example 2: negative fractions

Halfway between $-\dfrac12$ and $\dfrac13$. Common denominator $6$: $-\dfrac36$ and $\dfrac26$. Add: $-\dfrac16$. Halve: $-\dfrac1{12}$. It is just left of $0$, as expected, because $-\dfrac12$ is further from $0$ than $\dfrac13$.

Example 3: zooming in forever

Start with $0$ and $1$. Their midpoint is $\dfrac12$. The midpoint of $0$ and $\dfrac12$ is $\dfrac14$, then $\dfrac18$, $\dfrac1{16},\ldots$ Each step finds a new fraction between $0$ and the previous one. There is no end, so there are infinitely many fractions between $0$ and $1$.

Show the proof (optional reading for the curious)
It is a fraction. The sum of two fractions is a fraction, and halving is dividing by $2$, so $m$ is a fraction.
It is between. Since $p\lt q$: adding $p$ to both sides gives $2p\lt p+q$, so $p\lt\dfrac{p+q}2$. Adding $q$ to both sides of $p\lt q$ gives $p+q\lt2q$, so $\dfrac{p+q}2\lt q$.
Infinitely many. Repeat: put a new fraction between $p$ and $m$, then between $p$ and that one, and so on. We never run out. $\blacksquare$
Technique 10: find the fraction halfway between two fractions (the average)

Put both on a common denominator, add the numerators, then halve (multiply the denominator by $2$). Simplify at the end.

Example 1: Halfway between $\dfrac35$ and $\dfrac79$. Common denominator $45$: $\dfrac{27}{45}$ and $\dfrac{35}{45}$. Sum $\dfrac{62}{45}$, half $\dfrac{31}{45}$.

Example 2 (the number-line way): Halfway is the left value plus half the distance: $\dfrac14+\dfrac12\left(\dfrac56-\dfrac14\right)=\dfrac14+\dfrac12\times\dfrac7{12}=\dfrac14+\dfrac7{24}=\dfrac{13}{24}$. Same answer.

2

A second way: add the tops and add the bottoms (the mediant)

Scene 2: combining two shooting records

Back to the basketball final. Ana: $7$ out of $9$. Ben: $9$ out of $12$. If the two players are counted as one team, the team made $7+9=16$ out of $9+12=21$ shots. The team record $\dfrac{16}{21}$ lies between $\dfrac9{12}=0.75$ and $\dfrac79=0.78$: indeed $\dfrac{16}{21}=0.762\ldots$ A combined record can never be better than the best or worse than the worst player. This “add the tops, add the bottoms” fraction is called the mediant.

Theorem 5: the mediant lies between

If $\dfrac ab\lt\dfrac cd$ with $b>0$, $d>0$, then $$\frac ab\lt\frac{a+c}{b+d}\lt\frac cd.$$

What this theorem says, with examples

Example 1: the first step of a pattern

Between $\dfrac13$ and $\dfrac12$: the mediant is $\dfrac{1+1}{3+2}=\dfrac25$. Check: $0.333\lt0.4\lt0.5$ ✓. Between $\dfrac25$ and $\dfrac12$: mediant $\dfrac37$ ($0.4286$). Between $\dfrac25$ and $\dfrac37$: $\dfrac5{12}$. You can keep going forever, with new fractions each time.

Example 2: the team record

$\dfrac9{12}\lt\dfrac{16}{21}\lt\dfrac79$, as in Scene 2.

Example 3: a fraction with a small denominator

The mediant gives a fraction between $\dfrac3{7}$ and $\dfrac49$: $\dfrac{7}{16}$. It is easy to check: $3\times16=48\lt7\times7=49$ and $7\times9=63\lt4\times16=64$. (In Tab 5 you will see that no fraction between them has a smaller denominator than $16$.)

Show the proof (optional reading for the curious)
Start. $\dfrac ab\lt\dfrac cd$ means $ad\lt bc$ (Theorem 3).
Left inequality. $\dfrac ab\lt\dfrac{a+c}{b+d}$ means (cross-multiply) $a(b+d)\lt b(a+c)$, that is $ab+ad\lt ab+bc$, which is $ad\lt bc$. ✓
Right inequality. $\dfrac{a+c}{b+d}\lt\dfrac cd$ means $d(a+c)\lt c(b+d)$, that is $ad+cd\lt bc+cd$, which is $ad\lt bc$. ✓ $\blacksquare$
3

Fractions inside an interval

Technique 11: which integers $x$ make $\dfrac xn$ lie between two fractions?
  1. Multiply every part of the inequality by $n$ (a positive number, so the direction is kept).
  2. Turn the end values into decimals, or compare using whole numbers, to find the range for $x$.
  3. List the integers in the range, and check whether the end values are included (“between” usually means strictly between).

Example 1: Which integers $x$ make $\dfrac x5$ lie between $\dfrac{9}{4}$ and $\dfrac{17}{4}$? Multiply by $5$: $\dfrac{45}4\lt x\lt\dfrac{85}4$, that is $11.25\lt x\lt21.25$. So $x=12,13,\ldots,21$: $10$ integers.

Example 2 (the end value is a boundary): How many fractions $\dfrac n{15}$ lie strictly between $\dfrac14$ and $\dfrac23$? Multiply by $15$: $3.75\lt n\lt10$. So $n=4,5,\ldots,9$: $6$ fractions ($n=10$ gives exactly $\tfrac23$, which is not strictly between).

Example 3 (a single answer): Which integer $x$ makes $\dfrac x7$ lie between $\dfrac{22}{3}$ and $\dfrac{23}{3}$? Multiply by $7$: $\dfrac{154}3\lt x\lt\dfrac{161}3$, that is $51.33\lt x\lt53.67$. So $x=52$ or $53$. (Two answers: be careful about how many integers the question expects.)

Technique 12: place a fraction on a scale between two marked fractions

Use Technique 3: gap value $=\dfrac{\text{difference}}{\text{number of gaps}}$. Then divide the distance from the left mark by the gap value to find how many gaps along it sits.

Example: The marks are $\dfrac14$ and $\dfrac12$ with $10$ equal gaps. One gap is $\dfrac14\div10=\dfrac1{40}$. Where is $\dfrac3{10}$? Distance from $\dfrac14$: $\dfrac3{10}-\dfrac14=\dfrac1{20}=\dfrac2{40}$, which is $2$ gaps.

Step 5: Smart Thinking with Fractions

Competition problems about fractions rarely ask for brute force. This tab collects three smart ideas: what happens when you add the same number to the top and bottom, how to find the fraction with the smallest denominator in a gap, and how to count fractions in simplest form.

1

Adding the same number to the top and bottom

Scene 1: a student who is catching up

A student has handed in $3$ of $5$ homework tasks (a rate of $\dfrac35$). Each day she hands in one more task, and one more task is also set: so after $k$ days she has handed in $3+k$ of $5+k$. Does her rate go up or down? Surprisingly it goes up, and it creeps towards $1$ but never reaches it.

Theorem 6: if $0\lt a\lt b$ and $k>0$, then $\dfrac{a+k}{b+k}>\dfrac ab$

Adding the same positive number to the numerator and the denominator of a proper fraction makes it bigger, but still less than $1$. (For an improper fraction $a>b$ the effect is the opposite.)

What this theorem says, with examples

Example 1: which is larger?

$\dfrac{2020}{2021}$ against $\dfrac{2021}{2022}$. The second is the first with $1$ added to the top and the bottom, so it is larger. (Check with the gaps: $\dfrac1{2021}>\dfrac1{2022}$.)

Example 2: reaching a target

Add $k$ to the top and bottom of $\dfrac35$ so that it becomes $\dfrac79$: $\dfrac{3+k}{5+k}=\dfrac79$. Cross-multiply: $9(3+k)=7(5+k)$, so $27+9k=35+7k$ and $k=4$. Check: $\dfrac{7}{9}$ ✓.

Example 3: how many days to reach $95\%$?

$\dfrac{n}{n+5}\ge\dfrac{19}{20}$: $20n\ge19n+95$, so $n\ge95$. At $n=95$ the fraction is exactly $\dfrac{95}{100}$.

Example 4: an improper fraction goes the other way

$\dfrac53=1.67$ but $\dfrac{5+3}{3+3}=\dfrac86=1.33$. The fraction moved down, towards $1$. In both cases the fraction moves towards $1$.

Show the proof (optional reading for the curious)
Subtract. $\dfrac{a+k}{b+k}-\dfrac ab=\dfrac{b(a+k)-a(b+k)}{b(b+k)}=\dfrac{k(b-a)}{b(b+k)}$.
Sign. Here $k>0$, $b-a>0$, $b>0$, $b+k>0$, so the fraction is positive. So $\dfrac{a+k}{b+k}>\dfrac ab$. Also $\dfrac{a+k}{b+k}\lt1$ since $a+k\lt b+k$. $\blacksquare$
Technique 13: “the same number added to the top and bottom” problems
  1. Write the new fraction with an unknown $k$: $\dfrac{a+k}{b+k}$.
  2. Cross-multiply (Theorem 3) and solve the simple equation for $k$.
  3. Check by substituting back. For “at least” problems, solve the inequality and take the smallest whole number that works.

Example: What is the smallest whole number $n$ with $\dfrac{n}{n+7}>0.9$? $\dfrac{n}{n+7}>\dfrac9{10}$ gives $10n>9n+63$, so $n>63$. The smallest whole number is $64$. Check: $\dfrac{64}{71}=0.9014\ldots>0.9$ and $\dfrac{63}{70}=0.9$ exactly, which is not bigger.

2

The smallest denominator in a gap: neighbours

Between $\dfrac13$ and $\dfrac12$ the mediant is $\dfrac25$. Could there be a fraction between them with a denominator smaller than $5$? Check: denominators $2,3,4$ give $\tfrac12,\tfrac13,\tfrac14,\tfrac23,\tfrac34$ and none is strictly between $0.333$ and $0.5$. So $5$ is the smallest. Why? The fractions $\dfrac13$ and $\dfrac12$ are neighbours: $3\times1-1\times2=1$. The cross-product difference is as small as it can be.

Theorem 7: neighbouring fractions

Let $\dfrac ab\lt\dfrac cd$ with $bc-ad=1$. Then every fraction $\dfrac xy$ strictly between them has $y\ge b+d$, and the mediant $\dfrac{a+c}{b+d}$ achieves $y=b+d$ exactly.

What this theorem says, with examples

Example 1: $\dfrac3{7}$ and $\dfrac49$

$bc-ad=7\times4-3\times9=28-27=1$. So the smallest denominator of any fraction between them is $7+9=16$, and it is $\dfrac7{16}$. You can check by trying every denominator up to $15$: none fits.

Example 2: $\dfrac5{12}$ and $\dfrac37$

$bc-ad=12\times3-5\times7=36-35=1$. So the smallest denominator is $12+7=19$, with the fraction $\dfrac8{19}$.

Example 3: a pair that is not neighbours

$\dfrac14$ and $\dfrac12$: $4\times1-1\times2=2$, not $1$. The theorem does not apply, and indeed $\dfrac13$ sits between them with denominator $3\lt4+2$.

Show the proof (optional reading for the curious)
Setup. Let $\dfrac ab\lt\dfrac xy\lt\dfrac cd$. By Theorem 3, $ay\lt bx$ and $xd\lt cy$. All of these are whole numbers, so each difference is at least $1$: $bx-ay\ge1$ and $cy-dx\ge1$.
Combine. Multiply the first by $d$ and the second by $b$: $bdx-ady\ge d$ and $bcy-bdx\ge b$. Add: $y(bc-ad)\ge b+d$.
Use the neighbour condition. Since $bc-ad=1$, we get $y\ge b+d$. The mediant (Theorem 5) lies between and has denominator exactly $b+d$. $\blacksquare$
Technique 14: find the fraction with the smallest denominator between two fractions
  1. Compute $bc-ad$ for the two fractions $\dfrac ab\lt\dfrac cd$.
  2. If it is $1$, the answer is the mediant, with denominator $b+d$.
  3. If it is bigger, find a neighbouring pair between them (using mediants) and use the same idea, or search denominators in order.

Example: The smallest denominator for a fraction strictly between $\dfrac5{12}$ and $\dfrac3{7}$ is $12+7=19$, since $bc-ad=1$. The fraction is $\dfrac{5+3}{12+7}=\dfrac8{19}$.

Counting fractions in simplest form

By Theorem 2, a fraction $\dfrac ab$ is in simplest form exactly when $\text{HCF}(a,b)=1$. So counting simplest-form fractions is counting numbers that are coprime to $b$. This links back to the divisor ideas of 1.2.

Technique 15: count the fractions in simplest form
  • One denominator $b$: count the numerators $1\le a\le b-1$ with no prime factor in common with $b$.
  • All denominators up to $N$: list the denominators $2,3,\ldots,N$ and add the counts. Each simplest-form fraction is listed exactly once, because simplest form is unique (Theorem 2).

Example 1: Numerators for denominator $12=2^2\times3$: $1,5,7,11$ (not divisible by $2$ or $3$). $4$ fractions.

Example 2: all simplest fractions strictly between $0$ and $1$ with denominator at most $6$. $b=2$: $\tfrac12$ (1). $b=3$: $\tfrac13,\tfrac23$ (2). $b=4$: $\tfrac14,\tfrac34$ (2). $b=5$: four fractions. $b=6$: $\tfrac16,\tfrac56$ (2). Total $1+2+2+4+2=11$. In order: $\tfrac16,\tfrac15,\tfrac14,\tfrac13,\tfrac25,\tfrac12,\tfrac35,\tfrac23,\tfrac34,\tfrac45,\tfrac56$.
