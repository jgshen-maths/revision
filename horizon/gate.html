The great mystery

The secret rhythm of the primes

Primes look like chaos. 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13 … they jump around with no rule anyone has ever found. Yet a teenager once noticed that, in the large, the chaos obeys a law.

Carl Friedrich Gauss, probably around the age of fifteen, spent his spare time counting primes in long tables. He saw that near a number $x$ about one number in $\ln x$ is prime. So the number of primes up to $x$, written $\pi(x)$, should be close to

$\pi(x)\;\approx\;\dfrac{x}{\ln x}$

He could not prove it. It took until 1896, nearly a century, for Hadamard and de la Vallée Poussin to prove it independently, using tools from a part of mathematics that seems to have nothing to do with whole numbers. Look how well it already works:

Real count vs Gauss's guess

share of numbers up to $x$ that are primeGauss's guess $1/\ln x$

At $x=10^6$ about 7.8% of the numbers are prime, and Gauss's guess says 7.2%. The guess is a little low, but the shape is right and the relative error keeps shrinking.

Now a different kind of magic. In 1963 the mathematician Stanisław Ulam was sitting through a boring scientific talk and began to doodle: write 1 in the middle of the page, spiral outwards writing 2, 3, 4 … and circle the primes. The primes refused to scatter. They fell onto diagonal streaks, as if some hidden hand had drawn lines through the numbers.

Nobody fully understands every streak even today. Drag the slider and look for yourself.

And then there is the deepest door of all. In 1737 Leonhard Euler noticed that the Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic, the very theorem you proved this chapter, can be written as a single equation joining all the whole numbers to all the primes:

$\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac1{n^{s}}\;=\;\prod_{p\ \text{prime}}\frac{1}{1-p^{-s}}$

Left side: every integer, once each. Right side: every prime, once each. They are equal precisely because each integer factorises into primes in one way only. Bernhard Riemann in 1859 let $s$ be a complex number in this equation and found that the zeros of the resulting function seem to control the exact error in Gauss's guess. His claim that all those zeros sit on one straight line is the Riemann Hypothesis. It is unproved. The Clay Mathematics Institute offers one million US dollars for the answer.

And a smaller surprise from the same family: Euler also showed $1+\frac14+\frac19+\frac1{16}+\cdots=\frac{\pi^2}{6}$. The circle constant $\pi$ appears in a sum over whole numbers. Nobody expected that, and it is one reason mathematicians say number theory is never really only about numbers.

Where the map runs out

The frontier is closer than you think

In most subjects, the edge of knowledge is hidden behind years of study. In number theory you can stand at the edge in one afternoon.

And six primes, spaced 30 apart: 7, 37, 67, 97, 127, 157. Do such regular ropes exist as long as you like? In 2004, Ben Green and Terence Tao proved: yes, for any length you choose, there is a row of primes in arithmetic progression. Their proof borrowed ideas from a branch of mathematics that studies randomness. Tao was awarded the Fields Medal in 2006.

The proof shows that such ropes exist but does not give you a quick recipe to find them. The longest ones known are found by computers.

Frontier story 2 · The gap that would not close

Twin primes are primes two apart: (3, 5), (11, 13), (17, 19), (29, 31), (41, 43), (59, 61), (71, 73) … Do they go on forever? Nobody knows.

In April 2013 an almost unknown lecturer, Yitang Zhang, in his late fifties, shocked the mathematical world. He proved that there is some gap size, less than 70 million, which occurs between consecutive primes infinitely often. 70 million is not 2. But before Zhang nobody had shown any such bound at all. Within months a worldwide, open online collaboration (Polymath8) and a young researcher, James Maynard, pushed the bound down to 246. The wall has a crack in it. Maybe you will be one of those to widen it.

Frontier story 3 · Play with the 3n+1 problem

Pick any positive whole number. If it is even, halve it. If it is odd, triple it and add 1. Repeat. Every number anyone has tried eventually falls to 1. Does every number? Nobody can say.

Start at 27 and watch: the number climbs like a hailstone in a storm cloud, up to 9,232, before it finally crashes to 1 after 111 steps. Start at 97 and it takes 118 steps; start at 871 and it flies above 190,000.

This is the Collatz conjecture, posed by Lothar Collatz in 1937. Computers have checked every starting value up to beyond $10^{20}$ and all of them return to 1. Yet no proof exists. The great Paul Erdős is reported to have said of it, “Mathematics may not be ready for such problems.”

Pause on that. A rule a nine-year-old can follow, and every mathematician alive is standing in the same fog as you.

Why mention this in a chapter about integers? Because every step here is “even or odd?”, which is mod 2, and “times 3 plus 1”, which is arithmetic you can do in your head. The deepest difficulty hides inside the simplest operations.

Why the world needs the queen

Five secret chambers where number theory is already working

In 1940 the great number theorist G. H. Hardy wrote, almost proudly, that his favourite subject was useless: no war, no factory, no everyday life would ever be changed by it. History answered him with a laugh. Open the chambers one by one.

I
China · about 1,600 years ago

The General's Count

A famous Chinese text, the Sunzi Suanjing, asks: “There are things whose number we do not know. Count them in threes: two remain. In fives: three remain. In sevens: two remain. How many things?”

Later legend attached the trick to the general Han Xin, who is said to have counted his army without counting it: he simply asked the soldiers to line up by threes, fives and sevens, and looked at who was left over at the end of each line. Three small remainders told him the size of a whole army.

You know why this works: a remainder is a clock reading. Three clocks of sizes 3, 5 and 7 together can name every number up to $3\times5\times7=105$ without repeating. This is the Chinese Remainder Theorem. Today it makes computers do huge arithmetic quickly and keeps parts of cryptography fast.

All three clocks agree first at 23. Then again at 128, 233, 338 … always 105 apart, because $105=3\cdot5\cdot7$ is the first moment all three clocks come round together.

23 soldiers: 7 rows of 3 with 2 left; the pattern is the answer.

II
France · Christmas Day 1640

The Christmas Gift

Pierre de Fermat, a lawyer who did mathematics for pleasure, wrote a letter dated 25 December 1640 announcing a discovery. Which primes can be written as the sum of two squares?

Try a few: $5=1^2+2^2$, $13=2^2+3^2$, $17=1^2+4^2$, $29=2^2+5^2$. And the primes 3, 7, 11, 19, 23 … never work, no matter how you try. What separates the golden cards from the silent ones? The remainder when you divide by 4.

A prime is a sum of two squares exactly when it leaves remainder 1 on division by 4 (the prime 2, which is $1^2+1^2$, is the one exception to the pattern). The clock of size 4 sees something about primes that nothing else can. Euler gave the first proof about a century later, and the proof opens a door into a world where numbers like $2+i$ behave like primes. A whole new country: algebraic number theory.

III
Every second of your life online

The Padlock

When you open a banking page, your device and the bank have never met, yet they must agree on a secret code while strangers listen in. How is that possible? The answer is multiplication with a trapdoor.

Choose two large primes and multiply them. A computer does it in a blink. Now give the product to a stranger and ask for the two primes back. For numbers with hundreds of digits, the best known methods would take longer than the age of the universe. The rule you proved, that every number has exactly one prime factorisation, guarantees there is a single secret answer to find, and finding it is brutally slow.

In 1977 Ron Rivest, Adi Shamir and Leonard Adleman turned this into RSA. Its engine is the clock arithmetic of this chapter: Fermat's little theorem, extended by Euler. (In secret, the British mathematician Clifford Cocks had found the same idea at GCHQ in 1973. It was kept secret until 1997.) Hardy's “useless” subject now guards the world's money.

In 2020 a team factored a 250-digit number (RSA-250) after months on a huge cluster of computers, about 2,700 years of computing on one core. Real keys today have about 600 digits.

IV
A scratched disc that still plays

Songs Without Errors

You met the check digit. The ISBN on the back of a book is a remainder test: multiply the digits by 10, 9, 8 … 1, add, and the total must leave remainder 0 when divided by 11. One wrong digit and the test fails; the shop's computer instantly knows.

That is the baby of a giant family. Error-correcting codes do not only detect a mistake, they repair it. Reed–Solomon codes, built from arithmetic on finite clocks, work inside CDs, QR codes and the radio links to deep-space probes. A QR code can be partly torn or covered and still scan, because the missing digits can be recomputed from the remaining ones. A photograph from far beyond Neptune arrives intact because of the same mathematics that makes a number-theorist excited about primes.

V
A forest in North America

Nature's Calendar

Deep underground, certain periodical cicadas wait for 13 or 17 years, then emerge together in their millions. Not 12. Not 15. Primes.

One popular explanation uses the LCM you studied: a predator with a 4-year cycle meets a 12-year insect every 12 years, but a 13-year insect only every $\text{lcm}(4,13)=52$ years, and a 17-year insect every 68. Prime cycles avoid collisions with the shorter cycles of the world around them. Biologists still debate the full story. But the next time you compute an LCM, remember: evolution may have done it first.
