Step 1: Adding and Subtracting Fractions

In 2.2 you met the rational numbers $\mathbb{Q}$. Now we give them an arithmetic. The first rule of adding is simple: you can only count pieces that are the same size. Everything in this tab follows from that one idea.

1

Real life first: how long was the evening?

Scene 1: homework and reading

Ayla spends $\dfrac12$ of an hour on homework and $\dfrac13$ of an hour reading. How much time is that altogether?

It is tempting to say “$1+1=2$ pieces” but the pieces are different: a half-hour block and a third-hour block. Cut the hour into $6$ equal slices of $10$ minutes. A half is $3$ slices and a third is $2$ slices, so together we have $5$ slices of the same size: $\dfrac12+\dfrac13=\dfrac36+\dfrac26=\dfrac56$ of an hour, which is $50$ minutes.

The recipe is the same every time: rewrite both fractions with the same denominator, then add or subtract the numerators and keep the denominator. Theorem 1 turns this recipe into one formula that always works.
Theorem 1: adding and subtracting fractions

For integers $a,b,c,d$ with $b\ne0$ and $d\ne0$: $$\frac ab+\frac cd=\frac{ad+bc}{bd},\qquad \frac ab-\frac cd=\frac{ad-bc}{bd}.$$ If the denominators are already equal, this reduces to $\dfrac ab+\dfrac cb=\dfrac{a+c}b$.

What this theorem says, with examples

Example 1: Ayla's evening

$\dfrac12+\dfrac13=\dfrac{1\times3+2\times1}{2\times3}=\dfrac56$. ✓

Example 2: subtraction

$\dfrac34-\dfrac25=\dfrac{3\times5-4\times2}{4\times5}=\dfrac{15-8}{20}=\dfrac7{20}$.

Example 3: a negative fraction

$-\dfrac23+\dfrac14=\dfrac{-2\times4+3\times1}{3\times4}=\dfrac{-8+3}{12}=-\dfrac5{12}$. The negative part is bigger, so the answer is negative.

Example 4: why “add the tops and the bottoms” is wrong

$\dfrac12+\dfrac13$ is not $\dfrac25$. That number is smaller than $\dfrac12$, but we added something to $\dfrac12$. The mediant from 2.2 lies between two fractions; a sum is bigger than both (for positive fractions).

Show the proof (optional reading for the curious)
Same-size pieces. Multiply the top and bottom of $\dfrac ab$ by $d$ and of $\dfrac cd$ by $b$ (Theorem 1 of 2.2: equal fractions): $\dfrac ab=\dfrac{ad}{bd}$ and $\dfrac cd=\dfrac{bc}{bd}$. Both are now counted in pieces of size $\dfrac1{bd}$.
Count the pieces. $ad$ pieces plus $bc$ pieces is $(ad+bc)$ pieces, each of size $\dfrac1{bd}$. That is $\dfrac{ad+bc}{bd}$. Subtraction is the same with $ad-bc$ pieces. $\blacksquare$

Technique 1: use the lowest common denominator

The formula of Theorem 1 uses the denominator $bd$, which can be large. It is neater to use the LCM of the denominators (from 1.2). Rewrite each fraction with that denominator, add or subtract the numerators, then simplify.

Example 1: $\dfrac5{12}+\dfrac7{18}$. $\text{LCM}(12,18)=36$. $\dfrac{15}{36}+\dfrac{14}{36}=\dfrac{29}{36}$.

Example 2: $\dfrac7{15}-\dfrac3{10}$. $\text{LCM}(15,10)=30$. $\dfrac{14}{30}-\dfrac9{30}=\dfrac5{30}=\dfrac16$.

Example 3 (three fractions): $\dfrac29+\dfrac5{12}+\dfrac16$. $\text{LCM}(9,12,6)=36$. $\dfrac8{36}+\dfrac{15}{36}+\dfrac6{36}=\dfrac{29}{36}$.

Technique 2: mixed numbers

Two safe ways. (i) Change both to improper fractions, calculate, and change back. (ii) Add the whole parts and the fraction parts separately (for subtraction you may need to “borrow” one whole as $\dfrac bb$).

Example 1: $2\dfrac13+1\dfrac34$. Wholes: $2+1=3$. Fractions: $\dfrac4{12}+\dfrac9{12}=\dfrac{13}{12}=1\dfrac1{12}$. Total $4\dfrac1{12}$.

Example 2: $3\dfrac14-1\dfrac56=\dfrac{13}4-\dfrac{11}6=\dfrac{39}{12}-\dfrac{22}{12}=\dfrac{17}{12}=1\dfrac5{12}$.

Example 3: $5-2\dfrac38$. Borrow a whole: $5=4\dfrac88$, so $4\dfrac88-2\dfrac38=2\dfrac58$.

Does $\mathbb{Q}$ stay closed? And the laws we know

In Chapter 1 we asked whether a set is closed under an operation: do the answers stay inside the set? $\mathbb{Z}$ is closed under $+$, $-$ and $\times$ but not under $\div$. The rational numbers repair that failure, and we now check the first two operations.

Theorem 2: $\mathbb{Q}$ is closed under addition and subtraction, and the usual laws hold

If $x,y,z\in\mathbb{Q}$ then $x+y\in\mathbb{Q}$ and $x-y\in\mathbb{Q}$. Moreover $x+y=y+x$ (commutative), $(x+y)+z=x+(y+z)$ (associative), $x+0=x$, and every $x$ has an opposite $-x$ with $x+(-x)=0$.

What this theorem says, with examples

Example 1: closure

$\dfrac35+\dfrac{-7}{4}=\dfrac{12-35}{20}=-\dfrac{23}{20}$, again a fraction of two integers with a non-zero denominator.

Example 2: regrouping

Because of the commutative and associative laws we may add in any order and any grouping: $\dfrac37+\dfrac25+\dfrac47+\dfrac35=\left(\dfrac37+\dfrac47\right)+\left(\dfrac25+\dfrac35\right)=1+1=2$.

Example 3: opposites

$\dfrac79+\left(-\dfrac25\right)+\dfrac29+\left(-\dfrac35\right)=\left(\dfrac79+\dfrac29\right)-\left(\dfrac25+\dfrac35\right)=1-1=0$.

Show the proof (optional reading for the curious)
Closure. By Theorem 1, $\dfrac ab\pm\dfrac cd=\dfrac{ad\pm bc}{bd}$. The top $ad\pm bc$ is an integer because $\mathbb{Z}$ is closed under $\times$ and $\pm$. The bottom $bd$ is a non-zero integer, because a product of non-zero integers is non-zero. So the answer is in $\mathbb{Q}$.
The laws. $\dfrac{ad+bc}{bd}=\dfrac{cb+da}{db}$ because the integers satisfy $ad+bc=cb+da$ and $bd=db$. This gives commutativity. Associativity follows the same way from the associative law of the integers, by putting all three fractions on the common denominator $bdf$. The opposite of $\dfrac ab$ is $\dfrac{-a}b$. $\blacksquare$
Technique 3: regroup before you calculate

Look for terms with the same denominator, or terms that are opposites, or pairs that make exactly $1$. Move them together first (the laws allow it), and the work shrinks.

Example 1: $\dfrac{11}{13}+\dfrac27+\dfrac2{13}+\dfrac57=\left(\dfrac{11}{13}+\dfrac2{13}\right)+\left(\dfrac27+\dfrac57\right)=1+1=2$.

Example 2: $\dfrac12+\dfrac13+\dfrac16$. The pair $\dfrac13+\dfrac16=\dfrac12$, and then $\dfrac12+\dfrac12=1$.

Step 2: Multiplying Fractions, the Operator Way

In 2.2 a fraction acted as an operator: $\dfrac34$ of $20$ means shrink by $\div4$, then stretch by $\times3$. Multiplying fractions is simply applying one operator after another. This tab shows why the rule is so simple, and uses it for some beautiful chains.

1

Real life first: half of a third of a chocolate bar

Scene 1: sharing what is left

A chocolate bar has $\dfrac13$ left. You give half of that to a friend. What fraction of the whole bar does your friend get?

Cut the whole bar into thirds, then cut each third in half. You now have $6$ equal pieces of the whole bar, and your friend gets one: $\dfrac12\text{ of }\dfrac13=\dfrac12\times\dfrac13=\dfrac16$. The word “of” means multiply: apply the operator $\dfrac12$ to the number $\dfrac13$.

Notice that the answer is smaller than both $\dfrac12$ and $\dfrac13$. Multiplying by a fraction between $0$ and $1$ shrinks. This is the opposite of what happens with whole numbers, and it is the key idea of this tab.
Theorem 3: multiplying fractions

For integers $a,b,c,d$ with $b\ne0$ and $d\ne0$: $$\frac ab\times\frac cd=\frac{ac}{bd}.$$ In words: multiply the tops, multiply the bottoms. $\mathbb{Q}$ is closed under multiplication.

What this theorem says, with examples

Example 1: the chocolate

$\dfrac12\times\dfrac13=\dfrac{1\times1}{2\times3}=\dfrac16$.

Example 2: a fraction that stretches

$\dfrac32\times\dfrac43=\dfrac{12}{6}=2$. Both operators stretch (each is bigger than $1$), so the result is bigger than both.

Example 3: negative fractions

$\left(-\dfrac56\right)\times\dfrac9{10}=-\dfrac{45}{60}=-\dfrac34$. The operator $-\dfrac56$ shrinks and also flips direction on the number line. Two negatives give a positive: $\left(-\dfrac23\right)\times\left(-\dfrac94\right)=\dfrac32$.

Show the proof (optional reading for the curious)
Name the numbers. Let $x=\dfrac ab$ and $y=\dfrac cd$. By the meaning of a fraction, $bx=a$ and $dy=c$.
Multiply. $(bd)(xy)=(bx)(dy)=ac$. So $xy$ is the number that, multiplied by $bd$, gives $ac$. That number is $\dfrac{ac}{bd}$. Since $ac$ and $bd$ are integers and $bd\ne0$, the product is in $\mathbb{Q}$. $\blacksquare$
A picture. Cut a unit square into $b$ columns and $d$ rows, which gives $bd$ cells. Shade $a$ columns and $c$ rows. The overlap is $ac$ cells out of $bd$.

Technique 4: cancel before you multiply

Any factor on a top may cancel with any factor on a bottom, even in different fractions. Cancelling first keeps the numbers small and the answer already in simplest form.

Example 1: $\dfrac{14}{15}\times\dfrac{25}{21}$. Cancel $7$ ($14$ and $21$) and $5$ ($25$ and $15$): $\dfrac{2}{3}\times\dfrac{5}{3}=\dfrac{10}9$.

Example 2: $\dfrac{8}{9}\times\dfrac{3}{16}=\dfrac{1}{3}\times\dfrac{1}{2}=\dfrac16$ (cancel $8$ and $16$, then $3$ and $9$).

Technique 5: mixed numbers, powers and “of”
  • Change mixed numbers to improper fractions before multiplying: $2\dfrac14\times1\dfrac23=\dfrac94\times\dfrac53=\dfrac{15}4$.
  • A power of a fraction raises both parts: $\left(\dfrac ab\right)^n=\dfrac{a^n}{b^n}$. For example $\left(\dfrac23\right)^3=\dfrac8{27}$.
  • “$\dfrac23$ of $\dfrac35$ of $30$” means $\dfrac23\times\dfrac35\times30=12$. Do the multiplications in any order.
2

A chain of operators that collapses

Scene 2: nine operators in a row

Take $1000$ sweets. Take $\dfrac9{10}$ of them. Take $\dfrac89$ of what you hold. Then $\dfrac78$ of that, and so on, down to $\dfrac12$. How many sweets are left at the end?

Instead of nine calculations, multiply the operators first. In $\dfrac12\times\dfrac23\times\dfrac34\times\cdots\times\dfrac9{10}$, each top cancels with the bottom of the next fraction, like a telescope folding up.

Theorem 4: the telescoping product

For every positive integer $n$: $$\frac12\times\frac23\times\frac34\times\cdots\times\frac n{n+1}=\frac1{n+1}.$$

What this theorem says, with examples

Example 1: nine operators

$n=9$ gives $\dfrac1{10}$. So $\dfrac12\text{ of }\dfrac23\text{ of }\cdots\text{ of }\dfrac9{10}\text{ of }1000=\dfrac1{10}\times1000=\mathbf{100}$. (This is the competition question in the exercises.)

Example 2: a shifted chain

$\left(1-\dfrac12\right)\allowbreak\left(1-\dfrac13\right)\cdots\left(1-\dfrac1{50}\right)=\dfrac12\times\dfrac23\times\cdots\times\dfrac{49}{50}=\dfrac1{50}$.

Example 3: growing operators

$\dfrac32\times\dfrac43\times\dfrac54\times\cdots\times\dfrac{21}{20}$: everything cancels except the first bottom and the last top, giving $\dfrac{21}2$.

Show the proof (optional reading for the curious)
Write it all out. $\dfrac{1\times2\times3\times\cdots\times n}{2\times3\times4\times\cdots\times(n+1)}$.
Cancel. The factors $2,3,\ldots,n$ appear on top and bottom and cancel. Only the $1$ on top and the $(n+1)$ on the bottom remain: $\dfrac1{n+1}$. $\blacksquare$
Technique 6: spot the telescope

When a product of many fractions has the pattern “each top equals the previous bottom”, write the first few and last few fractions and cancel through. Rewrite $1-\dfrac1k=\dfrac{k-1}k$ to reveal the pattern. Remember that an operator chain is just a product, so the order does not matter.

Example: $\left(1-\dfrac12\right)\allowbreak\left(1-\dfrac13\right)\allowbreak\left(1-\dfrac14\right)\allowbreak\left(1-\dfrac15\right)\allowbreak\left(1-\dfrac16\right)=\dfrac12\times\dfrac23\times\dfrac34\times\dfrac45\times\dfrac56=\dfrac16$.

Step 3: Dividing Fractions and the Reciprocal

In Chapter 1 we found that $\mathbb{Z}$ is not closed under division: $7\div2$ left the integers. The rational numbers were invented to fix this. In this tab we see how, why “flip and multiply” works, and what happens when fractions are stacked inside fractions.

1

Real life first: how many glasses can we fill?

Scene 1: the juice jug

A jug holds $6$ litres of juice. Each glass takes $\dfrac34$ of a litre. How many glasses can we fill?

Think of it as an undo question. One glass is $\dfrac34$ L, so $n$ glasses is $n\times\dfrac34$ L. We want $n\times\dfrac34=6$. The operator $\times\dfrac34$ means shrink by $\div4$, stretch by $\times3$. To undo it we must shrink by $\div3$ and stretch by $\times4$, which is the operator $\times\dfrac43$. So $n=6\times\dfrac43=8$ glasses.

Dividing by $\dfrac34$ means applying the undo operator $\dfrac43$. That upside-down fraction has a name: the reciprocal. Theorem 5 shows this always works.
Theorem 5: dividing by a fraction is multiplying by its reciprocal

The reciprocal of a non-zero fraction $\dfrac cd$ is $\dfrac dc$, and $\dfrac cd\times\dfrac dc=1$. For $c\ne0$: $$\frac ab\div\frac cd=\frac ab\times\frac dc.$$ Every non-zero rational has a reciprocal, so $\mathbb{Q}$ is closed under division by non-zero numbers. Zero has no reciprocal.

What this theorem says, with examples

Example 1: the jug

$6\div\dfrac34=6\times\dfrac43=8$. ✓ Check: $8\times\dfrac34=6$.

Example 2: fraction by fraction

$\dfrac34\div\dfrac9{10}=\dfrac34\times\dfrac{10}9=\dfrac{30}{36}=\dfrac56$. Check by multiplying back: $\dfrac56\times\dfrac9{10}=\dfrac{45}{60}=\dfrac34$. ✓

Example 3: reciprocals of other numbers

The reciprocal of $5$ is $\dfrac15$, of $-\dfrac37$ is $-\dfrac73$ (the sign stays), and of $2\dfrac12=\dfrac52$ is $\dfrac25$. The number $0$ has none, because $0\times x=1$ is impossible. This is why we cannot divide by $0$.

Show the proof (optional reading for the curious)
What division means. $\dfrac ab\div\dfrac cd$ is the number $q$ with $q\times\dfrac cd=\dfrac ab$.
Test the candidate. Let $q=\dfrac ab\times\dfrac dc$. Then $q\times\dfrac cd=\dfrac ab\times\dfrac dc\times\dfrac cd=\dfrac ab\times1=\dfrac ab$ (Theorem 3 gives $\dfrac dc\times\dfrac cd=\dfrac{dc}{cd}=1$). So $q$ has the property that defines the quotient, and there is only one such number. $\blacksquare$

Technique 7: divide fractions and mixed numbers

Change mixed numbers to improper fractions, flip the second fraction (the divisor), multiply (cancelling first), and simplify. Always check by multiplying the answer by the divisor.

Example 1: $2\dfrac12\div1\dfrac14=\dfrac52\div\dfrac54=\dfrac52\times\dfrac45=2$.

Example 2: A ribbon $5\dfrac12$ m long is cut into pieces $\dfrac{11}{12}$ m long. $\dfrac{11}2\div\dfrac{11}{12}=\dfrac{11}2\times\dfrac{12}{11}=6$ pieces.

Example 3 (finding the missing factor): $\dfrac35\times?=\dfrac9{10}$. The missing number is $\dfrac9{10}\div\dfrac35=\dfrac9{10}\times\dfrac53=\dfrac32$.

Technique 8: negative powers of a fraction

From 2.1 we know $a^{-n}=\dfrac1{a^n}$: a negative index means “reciprocal”. For a fraction this flips it: $$\left(\frac ab\right)^{-n}=\left(\frac ba\right)^n.$$

Example: $\left(\dfrac23\right)^{-2}=\left(\dfrac32\right)^2=\dfrac94$. And $\left(\dfrac12\right)^{-3}=2^3=8$.

2

Fractions inside fractions

A fraction bar means divide, and it also acts as a bracket around everything above it and everything below it. A complex (stacked) fraction such as $\dfrac{\frac23}{\frac49}$ is just $\dfrac23\div\dfrac49$.

Technique 9: simplify complex fractions from the inside out
  1. Work out the numerator and the denominator separately, each as a single fraction (the bar is a bracket).
  2. Then divide: top $\div$ bottom $=$ top $\times$ flipped bottom.
  3. For nested reciprocals, start at the innermost fraction and work outwards one layer at a time.

Example 1: $\dfrac{\frac12+\frac13}{\frac12-\frac13}=\dfrac{\frac56}{\frac16}=\dfrac56\times6=5$.

Example 2 (nested reciprocals): $2+\dfrac1{3+\frac14}$. Innermost: $3+\dfrac14=\dfrac{13}4$. Its reciprocal is $\dfrac4{13}$. Then $2+\dfrac4{13}=\dfrac{30}{13}$.

Example 3 (a competition question): $\dfrac{20+\dfrac1{25-\frac1{20}}}{25+\dfrac1{20-\frac1{25}}}$. Inner layers: $25-\dfrac1{20}=\dfrac{499}{20}$ and $20-\dfrac1{25}=\dfrac{499}{25}$. Reciprocals: $\dfrac{20}{499}$ and $\dfrac{25}{499}$. Top: $20+\dfrac{20}{499}=\dfrac{20\times500}{499}$. Bottom: $25+\dfrac{25}{499}=\dfrac{25\times500}{499}$. The common factors $\dfrac{500}{499}$ cancel, leaving $\dfrac{20}{25}=\dfrac45$.

Notice the pattern in Example 3: top $=20\times\dfrac{500}{499}$ and bottom $=25\times\dfrac{500}{499}$. Seeing a common factor in the top and bottom saved a long calculation. Always look for one before you simplify.

Step 4: Mixed Calculations and Fraction Stories

Now we combine the four operations. You will learn the order of calculation with fractions, how to model a story with “fraction of the remainder”, and a powerful method for problems that run through several days: start at the end and undo.

1

The order of calculation with fractions

The rules from 2.1 do not change: brackets, indices, then multiplication and division left to right, then addition and subtraction left to right. A fraction bar and a root sign are hidden brackets.

Technique 10: calculate with all four operations

Write one operation per line. Multiplication and division first (after brackets), then add and subtract with a common denominator.

Example 1: $\dfrac12+\dfrac23\times\dfrac34$. Multiply first: $\dfrac23\times\dfrac34=\dfrac12$. Then $\dfrac12+\dfrac12=1$.

Example 2: $\left(\dfrac12+\dfrac23\right)\times\dfrac34$. Bracket first: $\dfrac76$. Then $\dfrac76\times\dfrac34=\dfrac78$.

Example 3: $\dfrac56-\dfrac14\div\dfrac38$. Divide first: $\dfrac14\times\dfrac83=\dfrac23$. Then $\dfrac56-\dfrac23=\dfrac16$.

2

Real life first: the shopping trip

Scene 1: spending in stages

Sam has some money. He spends $\dfrac13$ of it on a book. Then he spends $\dfrac14$ of the money he still has on lunch. He is left with $\$18$. How much did he start with?

The trap: the second fraction is $\dfrac14$ of the remainder, not of the original. After the book Sam has $\dfrac23$ of his money. After lunch he keeps $\dfrac34$ of that: $\dfrac23\times\dfrac34=\dfrac12$ of his original money. So $\dfrac12$ of the start is $\$18$, and he started with $\$36$.

“Fraction of what?” is the question to ask every time. When a fraction is taken of the remainder, use the fraction that is kept as the operator and multiply them.
Technique 11: the keep-fraction method
  1. For each stage, write the fraction that is kept ($1-\text{fraction spent}$).
  2. Multiply the kept fractions. This gives the fraction of the original that is left.
  3. Set that equal to the known amount and undo the operator (divide, then multiply) to get the start.

Example 1: A class of $30$. $\dfrac35$ are girls. $\dfrac23$ of the girls and $\dfrac14$ of the boys play sport. Girls: $18$, sport: $12$. Boys: $12$, sport: $3$. Total playing sport: $15$, which is $\dfrac{15}{30}=\dfrac12$ of the class.

Example 2: A tank is $\dfrac25$ full. After $36$ litres are added it is $\dfrac7{10}$ full. The $36$ litres is the difference $\dfrac7{10}-\dfrac25=\dfrac3{10}$ of the capacity, so the capacity is $36\div3\times10=120$ litres.

Example 3 (a trap): A price goes up by $\dfrac15$, then down by $\dfrac15$ of the new price. The operators are $\dfrac65$ and $\dfrac45$: $\dfrac65\times\dfrac45=\dfrac{24}{25}$ of the original, which is not the original price.

3

Start at the end: the cookie problem

Scene 2: a baker gives cookies away

Aerith bakes some cookies. On the first day she gives away $1$ cookie and then $\dfrac12$ of the remaining cookies. On the second day she gives away $2$ cookies and then $\dfrac12$ of the remaining. On the third day she gives away $3$ cookies and then none are left. How many cookies did she bake?

We know how the story ends, so we go backwards, undoing each step.

  • Day 3. She gives $3$ and has none left, so she began day 3 with $\mathbf3$.
  • Day 2. She ended day 2 with $3$. Giving away half of the remainder left $3$, so before that gift she had $3\times2=6$. Before giving $2$ cookies she had $6+2=\mathbf8$.
  • Day 1. She ended day 1 with $8$, so before the half-gift she had $16$, and before giving $1$ cookie she had $16+1=\mathbf{17}$.

Check forwards: $17-1=16$, half gone: $8$. $8-2=6$, half gone: $3$. $3-3=0$. ✓

To undo “gives away $\dfrac12$ of the remainder” (keep $\dfrac12$) we multiply by $2$. To undo “gives away $\dfrac18$ of the remainder” (keep $\dfrac78$) we multiply by $\dfrac87$. To undo “gives away $k$ cookies” we add $k$. Always undo in the reverse order.

Try it: the Backward Cookie Tracer

Technique 12: work backwards with undo operators
  1. Write the known end state first.
  2. For each step, from last to first, apply its undo: undo “give $k$” by adding $k$; undo “keep $\dfrac pq$” by multiplying by $\dfrac qp$.
  3. Check by running the story forwards.

The competition version. Each day $k$ she gives $k$ cookies and then $\dfrac18$ of the rest, for $7$ days, ending with none. Day 7: she begins with $7$. Day 6: the rest after giving $6$ must satisfy $\dfrac78\times\text{rest}=7$, so rest $=8$, and she began with $8+6=14$. Day 5: $\dfrac78\times\text{rest}=14$, rest $=16$, start $16+5=21$. The totals read $7,14,21,28,35,42,49$. She baked $\mathbf{49}$ cookies.

An elegant pattern. Each day she gives away exactly $7$ cookies in total ($k$ cookies plus $\dfrac18$ of the rest). Over $7$ days that is $7\times7=49$.

Step 5: Smart Thinking with Fractions

Some sums look terrifying, but each hides a pattern. In this tab we meet four big ideas: grouping into easy pieces, telescoping sums, pairing terms, and sandwiching a sum between two simple bounds.

1

Real life first: the thirteen-fraction puzzle

Scene 1: a sum without a calculator

A teacher writes this on the board and says “no calculators”: $$\frac17+\frac18+\frac19+\frac1{10}+\frac1{11}+\frac1{12}+\frac1{14}+\frac1{15}+\frac1{18}+\frac1{22}+\frac1{24}+\frac1{28}+\frac1{33}.$$

There are two good ways. Brute force: the LCM of all the denominators is $2^3\times3^2\times5\times7\times11=27720$. Writing each fraction over $27720$ gives numerators $3960,\allowbreak 3465,\allowbreak 3080,\allowbreak 2772,\allowbreak 2520,\allowbreak 2310,\allowbreak 1980,\allowbreak 1848,\allowbreak 1540,\allowbreak 1260,\allowbreak 1155,\allowbreak 990,\allowbreak 840$, which add up to $27720$. So the sum is $1$. That works, but it is heavy. Smart way: look for small groups that give a nice answer. We build the idea in Theorem 6.

Theorem 6: a family of unit fractions with a neat sum

For positive integers $n$: $$\frac1n+\frac1{2n}=\frac3{2n},\qquad \frac1n+\frac1{2n}+\frac1{3n}=\frac{11}{6n},\qquad \frac1n+\frac1{2n}+\frac1{4n}=\frac7{4n}.$$ In general, $\dfrac1n\left(1+\dfrac1{k_1}+\dfrac1{k_2}+\cdots\right)$: pull out the common factor $\dfrac1n$, and the bracket is a sum of small unit fractions.

What this theorem says, with examples

Example 1: a pair

$\dfrac19+\dfrac1{18}=\dfrac19\left(1+\dfrac12\right)=\dfrac19\times\dfrac32=\dfrac16$.

Example 2: a triple with ratio $1:2:4$

$\dfrac17+\dfrac1{14}+\dfrac1{28}=\dfrac17\left(1+\dfrac12+\dfrac14\right)=\dfrac17\times\dfrac74=\dfrac14$.

Example 3: a triple with ratio $1:2:3$

$\dfrac1{11}+\dfrac1{22}+\dfrac1{33}=\dfrac1{11}\left(1+\dfrac12+\dfrac13\right)=\dfrac1{11}\times\dfrac{11}6=\dfrac16$.

Example 4: pairs from different families

$\dfrac18+\dfrac1{24}=\dfrac18\left(1+\dfrac13\right)=\dfrac16$ and $\dfrac1{10}+\dfrac1{15}=\dfrac1{10}\left(1+\dfrac23\right)=\dfrac16$.

Show the proof (optional reading for the curious)
Factor out. $\dfrac1{kn}=\dfrac1n\times\dfrac1k$, so $\dfrac1n+\dfrac1{2n}+\dfrac1{4n}=\dfrac1n\left(1+\dfrac12+\dfrac14\right)$ by the distributive law.
Evaluate the bracket. $1+\dfrac12+\dfrac14=\dfrac74$, so the sum is $\dfrac1n\times\dfrac74=\dfrac7{4n}$. For $n=7$ this is $\dfrac14$. The other cases are the same. $\blacksquare$
Technique 13: group denominators that are multiples of a common number

Scan the denominators for ones related by simple ratios ($1:2$, $1:2:4$, $1:2:3$, $2:3$). Gather them, factor out the common unit fraction, and evaluate the small bracket. Hope for groups worth $\dfrac16$, $\dfrac14$, $\dfrac12$, whole numbers.

The thirteen-fraction puzzle. Groups: $\{9,18\}\to\dfrac16$, $\{10,15\}\to\dfrac16$, $\{8,24\}\to\dfrac16$, $\{11,22,33\}\to\dfrac16$, $\{7,14,28\}\to\dfrac14$. Only $\dfrac1{12}$ remains. Total: $4\times\dfrac16+\dfrac14+\dfrac1{12}=\dfrac23+\dfrac13=\mathbf1$. Both methods agree.

2

Telescoping sums and Egyptian fractions

Scene 2: the shrinking list

Can you add $\dfrac1{1\times2}+\dfrac1{2\times3}+\dfrac1{3\times4}+\cdots+\dfrac1{99\times100}$? One hundred terms! The secret is to split each term in two, so that most pieces cancel.

Theorem 7: splitting $\dfrac1{n(n+k)}$

For positive integers $n$ and $k$: $$\frac1{n(n+k)}=\frac1k\left(\frac1n-\frac1{n+k}\right).$$ In particular, $\dfrac1{n(n+1)}=\dfrac1n-\dfrac1{n+1}$, and therefore $$\frac1{1\times2}+\frac1{2\times3}+\cdots+\frac1{N(N+1)}=1-\frac1{N+1}=\frac N{N+1}.$$

What this theorem says, with examples

Example 1: a short sum

$\dfrac1{1\times2}+\dfrac1{2\times3}+\dfrac1{3\times4}=\left(1-\dfrac12\right)+\left(\dfrac12-\dfrac13\right)+\left(\dfrac13-\dfrac14\right)=1-\dfrac14=\dfrac34$.

Example 2: one hundred terms

$N=99$ gives $\dfrac{99}{100}$.

Example 3: gaps of $2$

$\dfrac1{2\times4}+\dfrac1{4\times6}+\dfrac1{6\times8}=\dfrac12\left(\dfrac12-\dfrac14+\dfrac14-\dfrac16+\dfrac16-\dfrac18\right)=\dfrac12\left(\dfrac12-\dfrac18\right)=\dfrac3{16}$.

Example 4: Egyptian fractions

$\dfrac16=\dfrac12-\dfrac13$ and $\dfrac1{12}=\dfrac13-\dfrac14$. Every unit fraction $\dfrac1{n(n+1)}$ is a difference of two unit fractions.

Show the proof (optional reading for the curious)
The identity. $\dfrac1n-\dfrac1{n+k}=\dfrac{(n+k)-n}{n(n+k)}=\dfrac k{n(n+k)}$. Dividing both sides by $k$ gives $\dfrac1{n(n+k)}=\dfrac1k\left(\dfrac1n-\dfrac1{n+k}\right)$.
Telescope. Using $k=1$: $\left(1-\dfrac12\right)+\left(\dfrac12-\dfrac13\right)+\cdots+\left(\dfrac1N-\dfrac1{N+1}\right)$. Every middle term appears once with $+$ and once with $-$, so only $1-\dfrac1{N+1}$ remains. $\blacksquare$

Pairing terms in an alternating sum

Scene 3: plus, minus, plus, minus

Find $x$ if $$\frac12-\frac14+\frac18-\frac1{16}+\cdots-\frac1{1024}=\frac x{1024}.$$ The terms are half of the previous in size and alternate in sign. Since the right side has denominator $1024$, multiply the whole equation by $1024$. Every term becomes a whole number: $$x=512-256+128-64+32-16+8-4+2-1.$$

Pair them: $(512-256)+(128-64)+(32-16)+(8-4)+(2-1)=256+64+16+4+1=341$. Each pair is a power of $4$, and $1+4+16+64+256=341$.

Technique 15: clear the denominators, then pair

If every denominator divides one number $D$, multiply the equation by $D$ to turn the fractions into integers. Alternating signs invite pairing neighbours. A list that multiplies by a fixed ratio (a geometric list) has a short formula; here we simply pair and add.

Example: $\dfrac13-\dfrac19+\dfrac1{27}-\dfrac1{81}$. Multiply by $81$: $27-9+3-1=20$. So the sum is $\dfrac{20}{81}$.

Scene 4: between which two whole numbers?

Which two consecutive whole numbers surround $S=5\times\left(\dfrac1{11}+\dfrac1{12}+\dfrac1{13}+\cdots+\dfrac1{20}\right)$? Adding ten fractions exactly is painful, and we only need a rough place. Replace groups of terms by simpler fractions that are a little bigger or a little smaller, so that we trap the sum.

Technique 16: grouped bounds
  1. Split the terms into a few groups.
  2. Lower bound: replace every term in a group by the smallest term of that group (the last one). Each group becomes (number of terms)$\times$(smallest term).
  3. Upper bound: replace every term by the biggest term of the group (the first one).
  4. Compute both bounds and read off the whole numbers.

Lower bound. Groups $\{\tfrac1{11},\tfrac1{12}\}$, $\{\tfrac1{13},\tfrac1{14},\tfrac1{15}\}$, $\{\tfrac1{16},\tfrac1{17},\tfrac1{18}\}$, $\{\tfrac1{19},\tfrac1{20}\}$.
