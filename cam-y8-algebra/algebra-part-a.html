Station A1 · Expressions and formulae

Writing expressions from words

The idea

A letter stands for a number we don't know yet. Words describe a set of operations, applied in order. To write the expression, apply each operation to everything you have so far.

“Multiply $n$ by 3, then add 8” gives $3n+8$.

“Add 8, then multiply by 3” gives $3(n+8)$. The bracket shows that the whole of $n+8$ is multiplied.

“Two-thirds of $y$, add 5” gives $\frac{2y}{3}+5$. Only $y$ is divided by 3.

Three words to keep apart:

WordWhat it isExample
ExpressionLetters and numbers, but no equals sign$3x-7$
FormulaA rule linking two or more quantities$v=u+10t$
EquationHas an equals sign, and can be solved for the unknown$3x-7=14$
Trap: $\frac{x+7}{5}$ means “add 7, then divide by 5”. “One-fifth of $x$, add 7” is $\frac{x}{5}+7$.
Play · the expression machine

Start with $n$. Add operations one at a time and watch the expression grow. Try the same operations in a different order.

Worked example

Lena thinks of a number $n$. Write an expression for her result when she:

  1. multiplies by 4, then subtracts 7. Multiply first: $4n$. Then subtract: $4n-7$.
  2. subtracts 7, then multiplies by 4. The whole of $n-7$ is multiplied: $4(n-7)$.
  3. takes two-thirds of the number, then adds 5. Two-thirds of $n$ is $\frac{2n}{3}$, so the result is $\frac{2n}{3}+5$.
  4. adds 5, then divides by 3. The whole of $n+5$ is divided: $\frac{n+5}{3}$.

Check with $n=6$: $4\times6-7=17$, $4(6-7)=-4$, $\frac{2\times6}{3}+5=9$, $\frac{6+5}{3}=\frac{11}{3}$. Same operations, different order, different answers.

Station A2 · Expressions and formulae

Substitution and changing the subject

The idea

Substitute means replace each letter with its value, then work it out using the order of operations: brackets, indices, then $\times$ and $\div$, then $+$ and $-$.

Put negative numbers in brackets: when $x=-3$, $x^2=(-3)^2=9$.

$3p^2$ means $3\times p^2$: square first, then multiply.

$Ah$ means $A\times h$.

The subject of a formula is the letter on its own, such as $y$ in $y=2x+t$. To make a different letter the subject, list what is done to that letter, then undo the steps in reverse order.

Trap: $-3^2$ and $(-3)^2$ are different. Always put brackets around the value you substitute.
Play · substitute step by step

Changing the subject with a flow chart

Worked example
  1. Find $2x^2-5$ when $x=-3$. Substitute: $2\times(-3)^2-5$. Index first: $2\times9-5$. Then multiply: $18-5=13$.
  2. Use $V=\frac{Ah}{3}$ with $A=6$ and $h=12$. $Ah=6\times12=72$, so $V=\frac{72}{3}=24$. (Writing $6+12$ is a common mistake.)
  3. Make $p$ the subject of $D=wp+4$. To get $D$ from $p$: multiply by $w$, then add 4. Undo in reverse: subtract 4, then divide by $w$. So $p=\frac{D-4}{w}$.
  4. Check: with $w=12$ and $p=8$, $D=12\times8+4=100$, and $\frac{100-4}{12}=8$. ✓

Station A3 · Expressions and formulae

Simplifying: like terms and index laws

The idea

Like terms have exactly the same letters raised to the same powers. Only like terms can be added or subtracted. Add or subtract their numbers and keep the letters:

Like termsNot like terms
$3x$ and $-5x$$3x$ and $3y$
$4a^2$ and $a^2$$a^2$ and $a$
$2ab$ and $7ba$$ab$ and $a^2b$

A term keeps the sign in front of it. In $5x+3y-2x+4y$, the $x$-terms are $5x$ and $-2x$, so $5x-2x=3x$. The $y$-terms give $3y+4y=7y$. The answer is $3x+7y$.

Multiplying terms: multiply the numbers, then multiply the letters. A power counts how many times a letter is multiplied:

$$\begin{gathered}m\times m=m^2\qquad 3m\times4mn=12m^2n\\[4pt] x^2\times x=x\cdot x\cdot x=x^3\end{gathered}$$

Dividing terms: divide the numbers, then cancel the letters that match:

$$\frac{12x^3}{4x}=\frac{12\cdot x\cdot x\cdot x}{4\cdot x}=3x^2$$

You use these every time you expand or factorise. Expanding $2x(3x-4)$ needs $2x\times3x=6x^2$. Factorising $6x^2-8x$ needs $6x^2\div2x=3x$.

Trap: $x+x=2x$, but $x\times x=x^2$. And $x^2+x^2=2x^2$, not $x^4$: adding like terms never changes the power.

Worked example
  1. $7a+2b-3a+b$. Group the like terms: $(7a-3a)+(2b+b)=4a+3b$.
  2. $5x^2+2x-3x^2-x$. $x^2$ and $x$ are different, so collect each kind: $(5x^2-3x^2)+(2x-x)=2x^2+x$.
  3. $4p\times3pq$. Numbers: $4\times3=12$. Letters: $p\times p\times q=p^2q$. So $12p^2q$.
  4. $15y^3\div5y$. Numbers: $15\div5=3$. Letters: $\frac{y\cdot y\cdot y}{y}=y^2$. So $3y^2$.
Why do these rules work? (the index laws)

Like terms. $3x+5x=(3+5)x=8x$. This is the distributive law used in reverse: taking out the common factor $x$. Terms like $3x$ and $5y$ have no common letter to take out, so they stay separate.

Multiplication law. For whole numbers $m$ and $n$: $x^m\times x^n=x^{m+n}$.

Proof. $x^m$ is $m$ factors of $x$, and $x^n$ is $n$ more factors. Multiplying puts all of them in one row, which is $m+n$ factors of $x$.

Division law. When $m>n$: $x^m\div x^n=x^{m-n}$.

Proof. Write $\frac{x^m}{x^n}$ with $m$ factors on top and $n$ underneath. Each $x$ underneath cancels one $x$ on top, which leaves $m-n$ factors.

What the laws do:

  • Numbers: $2^3\times2^2=8\times4=32=2^5$ ✓ and $10^5\div10^2=1000=10^3$ ✓.
  • Terms: $3m\times2m=6m^2$, $2x^2\times5x=10x^3$, $\frac{18a^3}{6a^2}=3a$.
  • Expanding: $3x(x^2+2)=3x^3+6x$.
  • Factorising: $12x^3-8x^2$ has HCF $4x^2$, because $\frac{12x^3}{4x^2}=3x$ and $\frac{8x^2}{4x^2}=2$. So $12x^3-8x^2=4x^2(3x-2)$.
  • Looking ahead to Year 9: $(x^2)^3=x^2\times x^2\times x^2=x^6$.

Station A4 · Expressions and formulae

Expanding brackets

The idea

Think of the number outside the bracket as an operator. In $3(x+5)$, the operator 3 stretches the whole length $x+5$ to three times its length. Stretching a path made of two pieces stretches each piece:

$$3(x+5)=3x+15$$

A negative operator also flips the direction, so every sign changes: $-2(x-3)=-2x+6$.

Multiply every term inside the bracket by the term outside. A grid (box method) keeps track:
$\times$$2x$$-7$
$3$$6x$$-21$
so $3(2x-7)=6x-21$.

A letter outside works the same way: $x(4x+1)=4x^2+x$. Then collect like terms: $6(a+7)-2(a+9)=6a+42-2a-18=4a+24$.

Trap: with a minus in front of a bracket, multiply every term by the negative number. $-2(a+9)$ gives $-2a-18$, not $-2a+18$.
Play · stretch the bracket

A letter as the operator: the area model

Worked example
  1. $4(3x-2)$: $4\times3x=12x$ and $4\times(-2)=-8$, so $12x-8$.
  2. $-3(2y-5)$: $-3\times2y=-6y$ and $-3\times(-5)=+15$, so $-6y+15$.
  3. $6(a+7)-2(a+9)=6a+42-2a-18=4a+24$.
  4. $3m(m+2)$: $3m\times m=3m^2$ and $3m\times2=6m$, so $3m^2+6m$.
Why does expanding work? (the distributive law)

Claim. For any numbers $k$, $a$ and $b$: $k(a+b)=ka+kb$.

Proof with lengths. Put a piece of length $a$ and then a piece of length $b$ end to end on a number line, giving a total length of $a+b$. The operator $k$ stretches every length to $k$ times itself. The first piece becomes $ka$ and the second becomes $kb$, still end to end. So the whole stretched length is $ka+kb$, and it is also $k(a+b)$. If $k$ is negative, every piece is flipped as well as stretched, so the same equality holds with both signs changed.

Proof with area. A rectangle $k$ wide and $(a+b)$ long splits into two rectangles, $k$ by $a$ and $k$ by $b$. The areas add: $k(a+b)=ka+kb$.

What it can do: mental arithmetic.

  • $6\times58=6(60-2)=360-12=348$
  • $7\times1.99=7(2-0.01)=14-0.07=13.93$
  • $25\times44=25(40+4)=1000+100=1100$

Station A5 · Expressions and formulae

Factorising: expanding in reverse

The idea

Factorising undoes expanding: $3(x+8)\;\to\;3x+24$ is expanding, and $3x+24\;\to\;3(x+8)$ is factorising.

Step 1. Find the highest common factor (HCF) of all the terms: the biggest number, and the highest powers of letters, that go into every term.

Step 2. Write the HCF outside the bracket, and inside it write what is left of each term.

Step 3. Check by expanding.

$12x^2-30x$: the HCF of 12 and 30 is 6, and both terms contain $x$, so the HCF is $6x$. Then $12x^2\div6x=2x$ and $30x\div6x=5$, giving $6x(2x-5)$.

Trap: $2(6x^2-15x)$ is equivalent but not fully factorised: 3 and $x$ can still come out of the bracket. Always use the highest common factor.
Play · find the common factor

Choose what to take out of the bracket:

Worked example
  1. $18x+24$. HCF of 18 and 24 is 6; $x$ is not in every term. So $6(3x+4)$. Check: $6\times3x+6\times4=18x+24$ ✓.
  2. $8h-4h^2$. HCF of 8 and 4 is 4; both terms contain $h$. So $4h(2-h)$.
  3. $14cd-7c$. HCF is $7c$. So $7c(2d-1)$. The 1 matters: $7c\times1=7c$.
  4. $12tu+16u-20$. The HCF is 4 ($u$ is not in the last term). So $4(3tu+4u-5)$.
Why does the HCF give the full factorisation?

Claim. If you take out the highest common factor $H$, the terms left inside the bracket have no common factor except 1.

Proof. Suppose the terms inside still shared a factor $f$ bigger than 1. Then every original term would be divisible by $H\times f$, which is bigger than $H$. That contradicts $H$ being the highest common factor. So nothing more can come out.

Examples.

  • $24x+32x^2$ can be written as $2(12x+16x^2)$, $4x(6+8x)$ or $x(24+32x)$. Only $8x(3+4x)$ is fully factorised, because 3 and $4x$ share no factor.
  • $15e+6i$: the HCF is 3 (no letter is in both terms), so $3(5e+2i)$.
  • Area use: a rectangle has area $12b^2-30b$ and width $6b$. Since $12b^2-30b=6b(2b-5)$, its length is $2b-5$.

Station A6 · Equations and inequalities

Constructing and solving equations

The idea

An equation is a balance: both sides have the same value. Do the same thing to both sides and it stays balanced. Aim to get the unknown on its own.

One unknown term: undo the operations in reverse order, like a flow chart. For $3x+5=17$: subtract 5, then divide by 3, so $x=4$.

Unknown on both sides: first subtract the smaller $x$-term from both sides. $5x+8=3x+20$ becomes $2x+8=20$.

Fractions: multiply both sides to clear them. $\frac{x+4}{6}=3$ becomes $x+4=18$.

Constructing: turn the words or the diagram into an equation first. For example, the two equal sides of an isosceles triangle give $3a-4=20$.

Trap: do each step to the whole of both sides, and check your answer by substituting it back.
Play · keep the balance
Do to both sides:

You can type an amount like 3x, x or 8.

    Worked example
    1. $3(x+4)=27$. Divide both sides by 3: $x+4=9$, so $x=5$. (Or expand first: $3x+12=27$, then $3x=15$.)
    2. $5x+8=3x+20$. Subtract $3x$: $2x+8=20$. Subtract 8: $2x=12$. Divide by 2: $x=6$. Check: $5\times6+8=38$ and $3\times6+20=38$ ✓.
    3. $\frac{x+4}{6}=3$. Multiply by 6: $x+4=18$, so $x=14$.
    4. “I multiply my number by 4 and subtract 1. I get the same as 3 times my number plus 6.” So $4n-1=3n+6$. Subtract $3n$: $n-1=6$, so $n=7$.
    Why can we do the same thing to both sides?

    Claim. If $a=b$, then $a+c=b+c$, $a-c=b-c$, $a\times c=b\times c$, and $\frac ac=\frac bc$ as long as $c\neq0$.

    Reason. $a$ and $b$ are the same number written two ways. Doing one operation to the same number gives the same result, whichever way it is written.

    Why not divide by 0? $2\times0=3\times0$ is true, but dividing both sides by 0 would “prove” $2=3$. Dividing by 0 has no meaning, so it is never allowed.

    Examples.

    • Rectangle: opposite sides $4(x+2)$ and 40 must be equal, so $4(x+2)=40$, $x+2=10$, $x=8$.
    • Isosceles trapezium with equal sides $7(x-2)$ and $3x+6$: $7x-14=3x+6$, $4x=20$, $x=5$.

    Station A7 · Equations and inequalities

    Inequalities and the number line

    The idea

    An inequality describes a range of values, not just one value.

    Symbol$<$$\le$$>$$\ge$
    Meaningless thanless than or equal togreater thangreater than or equal to
    Number lineopen ○closed ●open ○closed ●
    $-3<x\le2$ means $x$ is greater than $-3$ and less than or equal to 2. On a number line: an open circle at $-3$, a closed circle at 2, and a line joining them. The integers it allows are $-2,-1,0,1,2$.

    Reading in the other direction is fine: $8>y>2$ means the same as $2<y<8$.

    Trap: with $<$, the end value is not included. For $-3<x<3$ the smallest integer is $-2$, not $-3$.
    Play · build an inequality
    Worked example
    1. Write “$x$ is greater than or equal to 8 and less than 12” as $8\le x<12$.
    2. For $24<y\le27$: 24 is not included and 27 is. The smallest integer is 25, the largest is 27, and the integers are $25,26,27$.
    3. For $5.2<n\le8.75$, the integers are $6,7,8$. The ends are not integers, so look at the first and last whole numbers inside the range.
    4. $x>4$ is equivalent to $2x>8$: multiplying both sides by a positive number keeps the inequality the same way round.
    Why does multiplying by a positive number keep the inequality?

    Claim. If $a<b$ and $c>0$, then $ca<cb$.

    Reason. On the number line, $b$ is to the right of $a$. The operator $c$ stretches every distance from 0 by the same amount without flipping, so $cb$ stays to the right of $ca$.

    Preview. A negative operator flips the number line, so the order reverses. $2<5$, but $-2>-5$. You will use this in later years.

    Examples. $x<9 \iff 7x<63$. $y\ge1 \iff y+9\ge10$. $y\le1 \iff y-5\le-4$. Adding or subtracting never flips the sign.
