Station B1 · Sequences

Term-to-term rules

The idea

A sequence is a list of numbers in order. Each number is a term. A term-to-term rule tells you how to get from one term to the next.

$6.5,\ 7.2,\ 7.9,\ 8.6,\ \ldots$ has the rule “add $0.7$”. The next two terms are $9.3$ and $10$.

A rule can have two steps. First term 2, rule “multiply by 3, then subtract 1”: $2\to5\to14\to41$.

To go backwards, undo the rule in reverse order. The reverse of “multiply by 3, then subtract 1” is “add 1, then divide by 3”.

Trap: a term-to-term rule only links neighbouring terms. To jump from the 10th term to the 5th you must undo the rule five times. Halving the 10th term does not work.

Worked example
  1. $5\frac13,\ 5,\ 4\frac23,\ 4\frac13,\ \ldots$: each term is $\frac13$ less, so the rule is “subtract $\frac13$”. Next terms: $4$, $3\frac23$.
  2. In $1.3,\ 1.8,\ 2.3,\ 2.8,\ \ldots$, which is the first term greater than 15? The terms are $1.3+0.5k$. Since $1.3+0.5\times27=14.8$ and $1.3+0.5\times28=15.3$, the answer is $15.3$.
  3. First term 10, rule “subtract 4, then multiply by 2”: $10\to(10-4)\times2=12\to(12-4)\times2=16$.
  4. Backwards: the 5th term is 53 and the rule is “multiply by 2, then add 3”. Undo it: subtract 3, then divide by 2. The 4th term is $(53-3)\div2=25$ and the 3rd term is $(25-3)\div2=11$.

From patterns to position-to-term rules

The idea

A term-to-term rule is slow when you need the 100th term. A position-to-term rule goes straight from the position number to the term.

If the terms go up by the same amount $d$ each time, the rule is $$\text{term}=d\times\text{position}+\text{a fixed number}$$

Use a table. For $5,\ 8,\ 11,\ 14$ the difference is 3:

position1234
term581114
$3\times$ position36912

Each term is 2 more than $3\times$ position, so term $=3\times$ position $+\,2$.

Trap: the number you add is not the first term. For $3,\ 8,\ 13,\ \ldots$ the rule is $5\times$ position $-\,2$, because $5\times1=5$ and $5-2=3$.

Worked example
  1. A pattern of squares has 5, 8, 11 squares. The difference is 3, so start with $3\times$ position: 3, 6, 9. Each term is 2 more, so term $=3\times$ position $+\,2$.
  2. Check with pattern 2: $3\times2+2=8$ ✓.
  3. Pattern 25 has $3\times25+2=77$ squares. No need to draw 25 patterns.
  4. Kai's mistake: for $3,\ 8,\ 13,\ \ldots$ he wrote $5\times$ position $+\,3$. Check pattern 1: $5\times1+3=8$, not 3. The correct rule is $5\times$ position $-\,2$.

Station B3 · Sequences

Finding the $n$th term: what every letter means

The idea

The $n$th term is a formula that gives any term straight from its position. To find one, you must know what each part of $dn+b$ stands for.

position $n$$\color{#b5487a}{0}$1234
term$\color{#b5487a}{1}$591317

$n$ is the position number. It counts which term you want: 1st, 2nd, 3rd, … It is not a term itself. In $5,\ 9,\ 13,\ 17$, the 3rd term is 13, so when $n=3$ the term is 13.

$d$ is the common difference. Every time $n$ goes up by 1, the term goes up by $d$. Here $d=4$. That is why $d$ multiplies $n$: moving $n$ steps along adds $n$ lots of $d$. A sequence that goes down has a negative $d$.

$b$ is the “zero term”. Step back once from the first term: $5-4=1$. That is the term at position $0$, before the sequence starts. Every term is “the zero term plus $n$ steps of $d$”.

So the $n$th term is $\;dn+b=4n+1$.

The method, four steps:

  1. Find $d$: subtract each term from the next. Check the difference is always the same.
  2. Find $b$: zero term $=$ first term $-\,d$.
  3. Write $dn+b$.
  4. Check with two positions, for example $n=1$ and $n=4$.
Trap: $d$ goes with $n$, and $b$ is not the first term. For $5,\ 9,\ 13$ the answer is $4n+1$, not $5n+4$ or $4n+5$. Check: $4n+5$ gives 9 when $n=1$, but the first term is 5.

Worked example
  1. $8,\ 11,\ 14,\ 17,\ \ldots$ Step 1: $11-8=3$, $14-11=3$, $17-14=3$, so $d=3$. Step 2: zero term $b=8-3=5$. Step 3: $3n+5$. Step 4: $n=1$ gives $8$ ✓ and $n=4$ gives $17$ ✓.
  2. $7,\ 4,\ 1,\ -2,\ \ldots$ Step 1: the terms go down by 3, so $d=-3$. Step 2: $b=7-(-3)=10$. Step 3: $-3n+10$, usually written $10-3n$. Step 4: $n=4$ gives $10-12=-2$ ✓.
  3. $-15,\ -10,\ -5,\ 0,\ \ldots$ $d=5$ and $b=-15-5=-20$, so $5n-20$.
  4. $1\frac35,\ 3\frac35,\ 5\frac35,\ \ldots$ $d=2$ and $b=1\frac35-2=-\frac25$, so $2n-\frac25$.
  5. Using it: is 48 a term of $3n-5$? Solve $3n-5=48$: $n=17\frac23$. That is not a whole number, so 48 is not a term.
Why is the nth term always $dn+b$?

Claim. If a sequence starts at $a$ and goes up by $d$ each time, its $n$th term is $dn+(a-d)$. Notice that $a-d$ is exactly the zero term $b$.

Proof. To reach the $n$th term from the first, you add $d$ exactly $n-1$ times. So the $n$th term is $$a+(n-1)d=dn+(a-d).$$

What it can do.

  • $8,\ 11,\ 14,\ \ldots$: $a=8$ and $d=3$, so $3n+5$.
  • $-15,\ -10,\ -5,\ 0,\ \ldots$: $a=-15$ and $d=5$, so $5n-20$.
  • Finding a far term directly: the 50th term of $-14,\ -8,\ -2,\ \ldots$ is $6\times50-20=280$.
  • Comparing two sequences: when does $5n-20$ overtake $100-n$? When $5n-20>100-n$, that is $6n>120$, so from $n=21$.

Station B4 · Functions and graphs

Function machines and inverse functions

The idea

A function is a rule that turns each input $x$ into exactly one output $y$. A function machine shows the steps:

$x$→$\times2$→$-1$→$y$

As an equation: $y=2x-1$. A table of values lists some inputs and outputs: $x=3$ gives $y=5$.

The inverse function goes backwards: undo each step in reverse order.
$x$←$\div2$←$+1$←$y$
So the inverse function is $x=\frac{y+1}{2}$. Use it to find the input when you know the output.
Trap: order matters. “$+3$ then $\div4$” gives $y=\frac{x+3}{4}$, but “$\div4$ then $+3$” gives $y=\frac x4+3$.

then
Worked example
  1. Machine: $-4$, then $\div2$. Equation: $y=\frac{x-4}{2}$. When $x=10$, $y=\frac{10-4}{2}=3$.
  2. Inverse: undo $\div2$ first (multiply by 2), then undo $-4$ (add 4). So $x=2y+4$. When $y=13\frac12$, $x=2\times13\frac12+4=31$.
  3. Find the function from a table: $x=1,\ 3,\ 5$ gives $y=1,\ 11,\ 21$. When $x$ goes up by 2, $y$ goes up by 10, so $y$ goes up by 5 for each 1. Try $y=5x+c$: when $x=1$, $5+c=1$, so $c=-4$. The function is $y=5x-4$.
Why do we undo the steps in reverse order?

Claim. If a machine does step $A$ and then step $B$, its inverse undoes $B$ first and then undoes $A$.

Reason. Think of putting on socks and then shoes. To get back, you take off the shoes first, then the socks. The last step done is the first step to undo.

Examples.

  • $y=6x+1$ ($\times6$ then $+1$) has inverse $x=\frac{y-1}{6}$ ($-1$ then $\div6$).
  • $y=6(x-2)$ ($-2$ then $\times6$) has inverse $x=\frac y6+2$ ($\div6$ then $+2$).
  • $y=\frac x5+10$ has inverse $x=5(y-10)$.
  • Check any inverse by going there and back: $x=3\to y=6\times3+1=19\to\frac{19-1}{6}=3$ ✓.

Drawing a straight line from its equation

The idea

An equation such as $y=2x-3$ is a rule: for every $x$ it gives one $y$. Each pair $(x,\,y)$ is a point, and for an equation like this all the points lie on one straight line.

Four steps to draw it:

  1. Choose $x$ values, for example $-2,\,-1,\,0,\,1,\,2$.
  2. Work out $y$ for each one, using brackets for negatives: $2\times(-2)-3=-7$.
  3. Plot each point $(x,\,y)$: go across to $x$ first, then up or down to $y$.
  4. Join the points with a ruler, right across the grid.
$x$$-2$$-1$$0$$1$$2$
$y=2x-3$$-7$$-5$$-3$$-1$$1$
The points must line up. If one point is off the line, a $y$ value is wrong: go back and check that one.
Trap: $(3,\,1)$ means $x=3$, $y=1$: across 3, up 1. Plotting it as up 3, across 1 puts the point in the wrong place.

1. Fill in the table. 2. Click on the grid to plot each point (click a point again to remove it).

Worked example
  1. Draw $y=4-3x$ for $x$ from $-1$ to $2$. Table: $x=-1$ gives $4-3\times(-1)=7$; $x=0$ gives $4$; $x=1$ gives $1$; $x=2$ gives $-2$.
  2. Plot $(-1,\,7)$, $(0,\,4)$, $(1,\,1)$, $(2,\,-2)$. They line up, so join them with a ruler.
  3. Is $(3,\,-5)$ on the line? $4-3\times3=-5$ ✓, so yes. Is $(-2,\,9)$? $4-3\times(-2)=10\neq9$, so no.
  4. Where does it cross the $x$-axis? There $y=0$: $4-3x=0$, so $x=\frac43$.

Station B6 · Functions and graphs

Reading the gradient and $y$-intercept from a graph

The idea

Every straight line has an equation $y=mx+c$. You can read both numbers from the graph.

The $y$-intercept $c$: where the line crosses the $y$-axis (where $x=0$). Read the $y$ value there.

The gradient $m$: how much the line goes up for each 1 across.

  1. Pick two points where the line passes exactly through grid corners.
  2. Draw a right-angled triangle: across first (the run), then up or down (the rise).
  3. $$m=\frac{\text{rise}}{\text{run}}=\frac{\text{change in }y}{\text{change in }x}$$

If the line goes down as you move right, the rise is negative, so $m$ is negative.

Then write the equation. A line that crosses at $(0,\,2)$ and rises 1 for every 2 across has $m=\frac12$ and $c=2$, so $y=\frac12x+2$.

Trap: check the scale on each axis before counting squares. If one square is 2 units, a rise of 3 squares is a rise of 6.

Your equation: $m=$ $c=$
Work out $m$ and $c$ from the graph, then check.
Worked example
  1. A line crosses the $y$-axis at $(0,\,3)$, so $c=3$.
  2. It passes through $(0,\,3)$ and $(2,\,-1)$. Run: $2-0=2$. Rise: $-1-3=-4$ (it goes down 4).
  3. $m=\frac{-4}{2}=-2$.
  4. The equation is $y=-2x+3$. Check with the other point: $-2\times2+3=-1$ ✓.
Why do any two points on the line give the same gradient?

Claim. On $y=mx+c$, for any two points $(x_1,\,y_1)$ and $(x_2,\,y_2)$, $\dfrac{y_2-y_1}{x_2-x_1}=m$.

Proof. $y_2-y_1=(mx_2+c)-(mx_1+c)=m(x_2-x_1)$. Divide both sides by $x_2-x_1$ to get $m$. The $c$ cancels, so it does not matter which two points you choose.

What it can do.

  • Choose points far apart: the triangle is bigger and easier to count.
  • Fractional gradients: $(0,\,-1)$ and $(3,\,1)$ give $m=\frac{2}{3}$.
  • From two points you can write the whole equation. Through $(0,\,4)$ and $(2,\,10)$: $m=\frac{6}{2}=3$, so $y=3x+4$.
  • It is the same idea as the common difference $d$ in a sequence: the change for each step of 1.

Positive or negative gradient?

The idea

Always read a graph from left to right, like reading a sentence.

Line goes…GradientExample
uphill ↗positive$y=2x+1$
downhill ↘negative$y=-3x+4$
flat →zero$y=5$

From an equation: the gradient is the number multiplying $x$, with its sign, wherever it is written.

$y=10-5x$ has gradient $-5$. $y=7-x$ has gradient $-1$. $y=-3+2x$ has gradient $+2$.

Steepness is the size of the gradient, ignoring the sign. $y=-4x$ is steeper than $y=3x$, because 4 is bigger than 3.

Trap: the sign belongs to the $x$-term, not to whatever comes first. In $y=-3+2x$ the minus belongs to the 3, so the gradient is positive.

Play · sort the lines

Decide whether each gradient is positive, negative or zero.

Worked example
  1. $y=8-3x$: the $x$-term is $-3x$, so the gradient is $-3$ (negative, downhill). The $y$-intercept is $8$.
  2. $y=-6+x$: the $x$-term is $+x=+1x$, so the gradient is $+1$ (positive), even though the equation starts with a minus.
  3. A line through $(0,\,5)$ and $(5,\,0)$: going right, it goes down, so the gradient is negative: $\frac{-5}{5}=-1$.
  4. Which is steeper, $y=-4x+1$ or $y=3x$? Sizes 4 and 3, so $y=-4x+1$ is steeper (but it goes down).

Graphs in real life: which plumber is cheaper?

The story first

A pipe bursts at home. Two plumbers can come today.

  • Plumber A charges a $\$20$ call-out fee, plus $\$5$ for every hour.
  • Plumber B charges only $\$10$ to come out, but $\$8$ for every hour.

Which one should you call? B is cheaper to start with, but A is cheaper per hour. It depends on how long the job takes, and a graph shows the whole picture at once.

Each charge is a function: $\text{cost}=\text{rate}\times\text{hours}+\text{fixed charge}$. So A is $y=5x+20$ and B is $y=8x+10$.

The fixed charge is the $y$-intercept: the cost before any work is done.

The rate is the gradient: the extra cost for each hour.

Where the lines cross, both cost the same. Here $5x+20=8x+10$ gives $x=3\frac13$ hours. Shorter jobs: call B. Longer jobs: call A.

The same ideas work for any steady change: petrol in a tank going down (a negative gradient), two people walking towards each other, two plants growing.

Trap: on a distance–time graph, a steeper line means faster. Compare gradients, not where the lines end.

Worked example
  1. A bike costs $\$10$ to hire, plus $\$5$ per day. For $n$ days the cost is $y=5n+10$ dollars.
  2. 14 days cost $5\times14+10=80$ dollars, but 7 days cost $45$. So 14 days cost less than twice 7 days ($90$), because the $\$10$ fee is paid only once.
  3. An electrician charges $y=30t+50$. A bill of $\$185$ means $30t+50=185$, so $30t=135$ and $t=4.5$ hours.
  4. Temperature $T=70-6m$: the gradient $-6$ means the temperature falls by 6 °C every minute. The intercept 70 is the starting temperature.
